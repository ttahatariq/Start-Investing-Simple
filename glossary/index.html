Finance Glossary

Plain-English definitions for common investing and personal finance terms — no jargon explaining jargon.

45ABCDEFGHILMPRSTVZ

4

401(k)
An employer-sponsored retirement account that lets you contribute a portion of your paycheck, often before tax, into investments that grow until retirement. Read more →
403(b)
A retirement plan similar to a 401(k), typically offered by public schools, nonprofits, and certain government-adjacent employers. Read more →

5

50/30/20 Rule
A budgeting framework that splits after-tax income into roughly 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings or debt repayment. Read more →

A

APR (Annual Percentage Rate)
The yearly cost of borrowing money, typically used for loans and credit cards, generally without accounting for compounding. Read more →
APY (Annual Percentage Yield)
The actual amount a deposit account will earn in a year, including the effect of compounding — the standard figure for comparing savings accounts. Read more →
Asset Allocation
How your portfolio is divided among different types of investments — typically stocks, bonds, and cash — based on your goals, timeline, and risk tolerance. Read more →

B

Bear Market
A period of falling stock prices, commonly defined as a decline of 20% or more from a recent high. Read more →
Brokerage Account
An account that lets you buy, hold, and sell investments like stocks, bonds, and funds, separate from a bank account used for everyday spending. Read more →
Bull Market
A period of rising stock prices, commonly defined as a gain of 20% or more from a recent low. Read more →

C

Catch-Up Contribution
An additional amount people 50 and older are permitted to contribute to certain retirement accounts, beyond the standard annual limit. Read more →
Compound Interest
Interest earned on both your original investment and on interest that investment has already earned, causing growth to accelerate over time. Read more →
Credit Score
A number, typically 300-850, estimating how likely you are to repay borrowed money based on your credit history. Read more →
Credit Utilization
The percentage of your available credit (particularly on credit cards) that you're currently using — a key factor in most credit scoring models. Read more →

D

Debt Avalanche
A debt payoff method that directs extra payments toward the debt with the highest interest rate first, minimizing total interest paid. Read more →
Debt Snowball
A debt payoff method that directs extra payments toward the smallest balance first, prioritizing quick psychological wins. Read more →
Diversification
Spreading investments across many different assets to reduce the impact of any single one performing poorly. Read more →
Dividend
A portion of a company's profits distributed to shareholders, typically on a regular schedule. Read more →
Dividend Yield
A company's annual dividend expressed as a percentage of its current share price, used to compare dividend payments across stocks. Read more →
Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, rather than investing a lump sum all at once. Read more →

E

Emergency Fund
Money set aside specifically to cover unexpected expenses or income loss, kept safe and easily accessible rather than invested. Read more →
Employer Match
A contribution an employer adds to your retirement account based on your own contribution, up to a certain percentage of salary. Read more →
Envelope Method
A budgeting approach that allocates a set amount of money to specific spending categories, stopping when that category's "envelope" is empty. Read more →
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)
A basket of investments that trades on an exchange throughout the day, like a stock, often tracking a market index. Read more →
Expense Ratio
The annual fee a fund charges to cover its operating costs, expressed as a percentage of your investment. Read more →

F

FDIC Insurance
Federal deposit insurance that protects money in U.S. bank accounts up to a set limit per depositor, per bank, if the bank fails. Read more →

G

Growth Stock
Shares of a company expected to grow revenue and earnings faster than the broader market, often reinvesting profits rather than paying dividends. Read more →

H

High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA)
A savings account, often from an online bank, that pays meaningfully more interest than a standard savings account. Read more →
HSA (Health Savings Account)
A tax-advantaged account for medical expenses, available to those on a qualifying high-deductible health plan, which can also function as a retirement savings tool. Read more →

I

Index Fund
A fund holding a basket of stocks designed to track a specific market index, such as the S&P 500. Read more →
Inflation
The rate at which prices rise over time, which reduces the purchasing power of a fixed amount of money. Read more →

L

Lifestyle Inflation
The tendency for spending to rise in step with income, absorbing raises into a more expensive lifestyle rather than increased savings. Read more →
Lump Sum Investing
Investing a full amount of money immediately and all at once, rather than spreading it out over time. Read more →

M

Market Capitalization
The total value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated as share price multiplied by shares outstanding. Read more →
Minimum Payment
The smallest amount a lender requires you to pay on a debt each month, often barely covering accrued interest. Read more →
Mutual Fund
A pooled investment fund, similar to an ETF, but typically priced only once per day rather than trading throughout the day. Read more →

P

P/E Ratio
Price-to-earnings ratio: a company's share price divided by its earnings per share, used to gauge how expensive a stock is relative to its profits. Read more →
Pay Yourself First
A budgeting approach that automatically directs money to savings before any other spending, treating savings as a non-negotiable expense. Read more →
Pension
A traditional retirement plan where an employer promises a specific, predictable benefit in retirement and bears the investment risk. Read more →

R

REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust)
A company that owns or finances income-producing real estate, letting investors gain exposure to property through the stock market. Read more →
Required Minimum Distribution (RMD)
The minimum amount the IRS requires you to withdraw annually from certain tax-deferred retirement accounts, starting at a set age. Read more →
Risk Tolerance
Your capacity and willingness to endure fluctuations in investment value in exchange for potentially higher long-term returns. Read more →
Roth IRA
A retirement account funded with after-tax money, offering tax-free qualified withdrawals in retirement. Read more →

S

Sinking Fund
Money saved gradually for a specific, predictable future expense, like an annual insurance premium or holiday spending. Read more →
Social Security
A U.S. federal program providing retirement income based on lifetime earnings, funded through payroll taxes. Read more →

T

Target-Date Fund
A single fund that automatically shifts from more stocks to more bonds as a specified target retirement year approaches. Read more →
Traditional IRA
A retirement account funded with pre-tax money, offering an upfront tax deduction while withdrawals are taxed in retirement. Read more →

V

Value Stock
Shares of a company that appear undervalued relative to their fundamentals, often larger and more established with stable dividends. Read more →
Vesting
The process of earning full ownership of employer-contributed retirement funds over time, based on a plan's vesting schedule. Read more →

Z

Zero-Based Budgeting
A budgeting method where every dollar of income is assigned a specific job — spending, saving, or debt — until nothing is left unassigned. Read more →
