Stock split announcements often generate excitement and headlines, sometimes causing a stock's price to jump on the news alone. Understanding what a stock split actually does — and doesn't do — helps separate the real substance from the hype.

## What is a stock split?

A stock split increases the number of shares outstanding for a company while proportionally reducing the price per share, without changing the company's overall value (its [market cap](/blog/how-to-read-a-stock-quote/)) or your proportional ownership stake in it.

For example, in a 4-for-1 stock split, each existing share becomes 4 shares, and the price per share is divided by 4. If you owned 10 shares worth $400 each ($4,000 total) before the split, you'd own 40 shares worth $100 each ($4,000 total) after — the same total value, just divided into more, cheaper shares.

## What a stock split does NOT do

This is the most important thing to understand: **a stock split does not create any new value**. The company's underlying business, earnings, and total market value are unchanged. It's often compared to cutting a pizza into more slices — you have more pieces, but not more pizza.

## Why do companies do stock splits at all?

If a split doesn't change underlying value, why bother? A few common reasons:

- **Improving accessibility.** A lower per-share price can make a stock feel more accessible to retail investors, particularly those without access to fractional shares, since a full share becomes more affordable at a lower nominal price.
- **Psychological and liquidity effects.** Some research suggests stocks can see increased trading activity after a split, potentially due to the perception of increased affordability, though this doesn't reflect any actual change in the company's value.
- **Signaling confidence.** A split can sometimes be interpreted by the market as a signal that company leadership is confident about continued growth, though this interpretation isn't guaranteed and shouldn't be treated as reliable investment information on its own.

## Reverse stock splits: the opposite scenario

A reverse stock split works in the opposite direction: it reduces the number of shares outstanding while proportionally increasing the price per share. For example, in a 1-for-10 reverse split, 10 shares become 1 share, and the price multiplies by 10 accordingly.

Reverse splits are sometimes used by companies whose share price has fallen significantly, in part to meet minimum price requirements for continued listing on certain stock exchanges. Unlike regular splits, reverse splits are sometimes (though not always) associated with underlying business struggles, since a very low share price often reflects declining investor confidence — though a reverse split itself is simply a mechanical adjustment, not inherently a sign of trouble on its own.

## Why stock price alone shouldn't drive investment decisions

Since a split doesn't change underlying value, and as discussed in our guide on [reading a stock quote](/blog/how-to-read-a-stock-quote/), share price alone tells you very little about whether an investment is attractively valued. A $50 stock isn't inherently "cheaper" or "better value" than a $500 stock — what matters is price relative to the company's earnings, growth prospects, and overall size, not the raw share price.

## How splits affect existing shareholders

If you already own shares when a split occurs, your total investment value doesn't change — you simply end up with more shares at a proportionally lower price each. No action is typically required from shareholders; the adjustment happens automatically in your brokerage account.

## Key takeaways

- A stock split increases the number of shares and proportionally decreases the price per share, without changing the company's total value or your ownership stake.
- Splits are often done to improve perceived accessibility or signal confidence, not because of any change in underlying business fundamentals.
- Reverse splits work in the opposite direction and are sometimes (not always) associated with a company trying to meet exchange listing requirements after a price decline.
- Share price alone — before or after a split — isn't a meaningful measure of value on its own.
