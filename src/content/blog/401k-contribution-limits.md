The **401(k) max contribution for 2027 is $25,500**, up $1,000 from $24,500 in 2026. If you're 50 or older, you can contribute an extra **$8,500** (for $34,000 total), and if you're between **60 and 63**, a larger **$11,750** catch-up applies instead (for $37,250 total). These limits were announced by the IRS on November 12, 2026, and apply to contributions taken from paychecks during calendar year 2027.

If you're new to how these plans work, start with our guide to [what a 401(k) is and how it works](/blog/what-is-a-401k/). Below is every limit that matters, what counts toward each one, and how to turn the annual number into a per-paycheck amount.

## 2027 401(k) limits at a glance

| Limit | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Employee contribution (401(k), 403(b), most 457(b), TSP) | $24,500 | **$25,500** |
| Catch-up contribution, age 50+ | $8,000 | **$8,500** |
| Total you can contribute at 50+ | $32,500 | **$34,000** |
| "Super" catch-up, ages 60–63 | $11,250 | **$11,750** |
| Total you can contribute at 60–63 | $35,750 | **$37,250** |
| Total limit, employee + employer (excluding catch-up) | $72,000 | **$75,000** |
| SIMPLE IRA employee contribution | $17,000 | **$17,500** |
| IRA contribution (Traditional + Roth combined) | $7,500 | **$7,500** |
| IRA catch-up, age 50+ | $1,100 | **$1,100** |

*Source: IRS news release IR-2026-136 and Notice 2026-66. Always confirm current figures at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits).*

## The three limits you need to know

### 1. The employee contribution limit: $25,500

This is the number most people mean when they say "maxing out" a 401(k). It covers everything **you** contribute from your paycheck, and a few rules apply:

- **Traditional and Roth share it.** Your [Traditional and Roth 401(k)](/blog/traditional-401k-vs-roth-401k/) contributions count toward the same $25,500. Splitting between them doesn't increase your limit.
- **It's per person, not per plan.** If you change jobs mid-year or have two employers, your combined contributions across every 401(k) and 403(b) can't exceed $25,500. Your new employer has no way of knowing what you contributed at your last job, so tracking this is your responsibility.
- **403(b) plans use the same limit.** If you work for a school or nonprofit, see [how a 403(b) compares to a 401(k)](/blog/403b-vs-401k/). The contribution limits are the same.
- **Governmental 457(b) plans are separate.** If you have access to both a 403(b) and a 457(b), you can generally contribute up to the full limit in each.

### 2. Catch-up contributions: $8,500 or $11,750

If you turn 50 at any point during 2027, you can contribute an additional $8,500 on top of the standard limit. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, people who turn **60, 61, 62, or 63** during the year get a higher catch-up of $11,750 instead, if their plan offers it. At 64, you go back to the standard age-50 catch-up.

One newer rule to know: starting in 2026, **higher earners must make catch-up contributions as Roth (after-tax) contributions**. This applies if your prior-year wages from that employer were above an inflation-adjusted threshold (around $150,000 when the rule started). Your plan administrator applies this automatically, but it changes the tax treatment of that money.

Our [catch-up contributions guide](/blog/catch-up-contributions-after-50/) covers how to use these extra years well.

### 3. The total limit: $75,000

There's a separate, much higher cap on **all** money going into your 401(k) in a year: your contributions, your employer's match, and any profit-sharing contributions. For 2027, that total is $75,000 (catch-up contributions are allowed on top of it).

Most employees never come close to this. It matters mainly if you:

- Have a very generous employer contribution
- Are self-employed with a Solo 401(k), where you contribute as both "employee" and "employer" (see our guide to [retirement accounts for the self-employed](/blog/retirement-accounts-for-self-employed/))
- Have a plan that allows after-tax contributions beyond the $25,500 limit

**Your employer match does not reduce your $25,500.** If you contribute $25,500 and your employer adds $6,000, you've contributed the maximum and your total is $31,500, well under $75,000.

## What the 2027 max looks like per paycheck

To hit exactly $25,500 by the end of 2027, contribute roughly:

| Pay schedule | Paychecks per year | Per paycheck |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | 52 | $490.38 |
| Every two weeks | 26 | $980.77 |
| Twice a month | 24 | $1,062.50 |
| Monthly | 12 | $2,125.00 |

Most plans ask for a **percentage** of your pay rather than a dollar amount. To convert, divide $25,500 by your salary. On an $85,000 salary, that's 30%. On a $120,000 salary, it's about 21.3%.

### Watch out for front-loading and the match

If you contribute so aggressively that you hit $25,500 by, say, September, your contributions stop for the rest of the year. If your employer calculates the match **per paycheck** and doesn't do a year-end "true-up," you can lose the match for those remaining months. Check your plan's rules before front-loading. Spreading contributions evenly across the year is the safer default.

## Should you actually contribute the max?

Knowing the limit doesn't mean you need to hit it. $25,500 is about 30% of an $85,000 salary, and for most people that isn't realistic or even the best use of every dollar. A common order of priorities is:

1. Contribute enough to get your **full employer match**.
2. Build an [emergency fund](/blog/build-emergency-fund-before-investing/).
3. Pay off high-interest debt.
4. Increase your contribution rate over time, toward 15% of income (including the match).
5. Max out if your budget allows.

For a realistic number based on your age and income, see [how much you should contribute to your 401(k)](/blog/how-much-to-contribute-to-401k-by-age/), and for the trade-offs of going all the way, read [should you max out your 401(k)?](/blog/should-you-max-out-your-401k/)

## How the 401(k) limit compares to an IRA

The IRA limit stays at **$7,500 for 2027** ($8,600 if you're 50+). It's completely separate from your 401(k) limit, so you can contribute to both in the same year. Someone under 50 could put $25,500 in a 401(k) **and** $7,500 in an IRA, for $33,000 total.

Choosing between them, or using both, often comes down to Roth vs. Traditional. Our [Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA](/blog/roth-401k-vs-roth-ira/) comparison walks through the differences, including why a Roth IRA has income limits and a Roth 401(k) doesn't.

## What if you contribute too much?

Over-contributing usually happens after a job change, when two employers each withhold up to the limit. If it happens:

- Tell your plan administrator as soon as you notice.
- The excess, plus any earnings on it, generally needs to be withdrawn **by April 15, 2028** (for 2027 contributions).
- If it isn't corrected in time, the excess can be taxed twice: once in the year you contributed and again when it's eventually withdrawn.

## Key takeaways

- The 2027 401(k) employee limit is **$25,500** ($34,000 at 50+, $37,250 at ages 60–63).
- Traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions **share** the same limit, and the limit applies **per person** across all your 401(k) and 403(b) plans.
- Your employer match doesn't count toward $25,500; it counts toward the separate **$75,000** total limit.
- The IRA limit is separate ($7,500), so you can contribute to both.
