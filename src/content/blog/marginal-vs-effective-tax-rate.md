"I turned down a raise because it would push me into a higher tax bracket" is a genuinely common misunderstanding — and one that can lead to real, unnecessary financial decisions based on a misconception about how income tax brackets actually work.

## How tax brackets actually work

Under a progressive tax system, income is taxed in layers, or brackets, with each successive layer of income taxed at a higher rate — but **only the income within that specific bracket** is taxed at that bracket's rate. Moving into a higher bracket doesn't retroactively apply that higher rate to all of your income.

## A simplified illustration

Imagine a simplified tax system with these brackets:

- 10% on income up to $10,000
- 20% on income from $10,000 to $40,000
- 30% on income above $40,000

If you earn $45,000, you don't pay 30% on the full $45,000. Instead:

- The first $10,000 is taxed at 10% = $1,000
- The next $30,000 (from $10,000 to $40,000) is taxed at 20% = $6,000
- The remaining $5,000 (above $40,000) is taxed at 30% = $1,500

Total tax: $8,500 — not $13,500 (which is what a flat 30% on the full amount would incorrectly suggest).

## Marginal tax rate

Your **marginal tax rate** is the rate applied to your *last* dollar of income — in the example above, 30%, since that's the bracket your top slice of income falls into. This is the rate most commonly referenced when people talk about "my tax bracket."

## Effective tax rate

Your **effective tax rate** is your *total* tax divided by your *total* income — the real, blended average rate you actually paid. In the example above: $8,500 ÷ $45,000 ≈ 18.9%. Notice this is meaningfully lower than the 30% marginal rate, because most of the income was taxed at the lower brackets underneath it.

## Why this distinction matters

- **A raise never reduces your take-home pay** under this system. Since only the additional income above your previous top bracket gets taxed at a higher rate, you always keep more from a raise than you did without it — the "it's not worth it because of taxes" concern about raises is generally based on a misunderstanding of how brackets work.
- **Your effective rate is usually the more meaningful number** for understanding your overall tax burden, since it reflects what you actually paid across all your income, not just the rate on the top slice.
- **Deductions and credits interact with this system too** — reducing your taxable income can lower how much of your income falls into higher brackets, which is part of why understanding your bracket structure matters for tax planning generally.

## Where this gets more complicated

Real tax systems include additional factors this simplified illustration doesn't cover — different filing statuses, various deductions and credits, and sometimes different treatment for certain types of income (like [dividends](/blog/what-are-dividends/) or long-term investment gains). The core principle (bracket layers apply only to the income within them) holds, but the exact numbers require checking current, official tax bracket figures for your specific situation.

## The bottom line

Income tax brackets apply in layers, not as a single flat rate on all your income once you cross a threshold — meaning your marginal rate (the rate on your top slice) and your effective rate (your real average rate) are two different, both useful numbers. Understanding the difference clears up one of the most common and persistent misunderstandings about how taxes actually work.
