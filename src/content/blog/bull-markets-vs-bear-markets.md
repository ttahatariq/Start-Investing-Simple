Financial news constantly references "bull markets" and "bear markets," often with dramatic framing. Understanding what these terms actually mean — and what they don't — can help you interpret market news more calmly and accurately.

## What is a bull market?

A bull market generally refers to a period when stock prices are rising, or expected to rise, typically accompanied by investor optimism and confidence. While there's no single universal definition, a common benchmark is a rise of 20% or more from a recent low, sustained over a meaningful period.

Bull markets are often associated with:
- Economic growth and rising corporate profits
- Increasing investor confidence and willingness to buy
- Generally positive sentiment in financial media

## What is a bear market?

A bear market is essentially the opposite: a period of falling stock prices, typically defined as a decline of 20% or more from a recent high, often accompanied by widespread pessimism and reduced investor confidence.

Bear markets are often associated with:
- Economic slowdowns or recessions
- Declining corporate profits or negative earnings outlooks
- Increased investor fear, sometimes leading to panic selling

## Where do the terms "bull" and "bear" come from?

The exact origin is debated, but a commonly cited explanation relates to how each animal attacks: a bull thrusts its horns upward, while a bear swipes its paws downward — a rough visual metaphor for rising versus falling markets. Regardless of the precise etymology, the terms have become standard market vocabulary.

## How long do bull and bear markets typically last?

Historically, bull markets have generally lasted considerably longer than bear markets, though duration varies significantly and there's no fixed rule for how long either will last in any specific instance. Some bear markets have been sharp and relatively short-lived; others have persisted for extended periods. Because of this variability, treating any given bull or bear market's timeline as predictable is generally not a reliable strategy.

## Why this matters for long-term investors

It's easy to feel that a bear market is a signal to exit investing entirely, and that a bull market is confirmation that everything is fine. In practice:

- **Bear markets have historically been followed by recoveries** — though this is based on historical patterns, not a guarantee for any future bear market.
- **Trying to exit before a bear market and re-enter before the next bull market** requires accurately predicting both events, which — as discussed in our [market volatility guide](/blog/understanding-market-volatility/) — is extraordinarily difficult to do consistently.
- **Long-term investors with a properly matched [asset allocation](/blog/risk-tolerance-and-asset-allocation/)** are generally better positioned to ride out both bull and bear markets without needing to react to short-term labels in the news.

## A word of caution about market "calls"

Financial commentators frequently declare the start or end of bull and bear markets, sometimes well after the fact (since the 20% threshold can only be confirmed in hindsight) and sometimes as speculative predictions. It's worth treating these declarations as descriptive labels for what has already happened, or speculative forecasts about what might happen — not as reliable, actionable trading signals.

## Bull and bear markets in different asset classes

While the terms are most commonly used for stock markets, they can apply to other markets too — bond markets, real estate, commodities, and even individual sectors can be described as being in a "bull" or "bear" phase. The same core idea applies: sustained rising prices versus sustained falling prices, relative to a recent high or low.

## Key takeaways

- A bull market describes rising prices and investor optimism; a bear market describes falling prices and pessimism, each commonly defined around a 20% move from a recent extreme.
- Bull and bear market durations vary significantly and aren't predictable in advance.
- Long-term investors generally benefit more from maintaining a well-matched asset allocation than from trying to react to bull/bear market declarations in the news.
