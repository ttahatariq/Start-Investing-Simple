Real estate has long been considered a popular way to build wealth, but buying property directly requires significant capital, ongoing management, and isn't easy to sell quickly if you need the money. A REIT offers a way to invest in real estate without any of that.

## What is a REIT?

A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust) is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate — office buildings, apartment complexes, shopping centers, warehouses, data centers, and more. Shares of publicly traded REITs are bought and sold on stock exchanges just like regular stocks, giving investors a way to gain exposure to real estate without directly owning or managing property themselves.

By law, in the U.S., REITs are required to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders as dividends, which is why REITs are generally known for relatively high dividend yields compared to many other types of stocks.

## Types of REITs

- **Equity REITs**: Own and operate physical real estate directly, earning income primarily through rent. This is the most common type.
- **Mortgage REITs**: Provide financing for real estate by purchasing or originating mortgages, earning income from interest rather than rent.
- **Specialty REITs**: Focus on specific property types like data centers, cell towers, healthcare facilities, or self-storage — sectors that have grown in popularity as distinct REIT categories in recent years.

## REITs vs. buying property directly

| | REITs | Direct property ownership |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital required** | Can start with the price of a single share | Typically requires a large down payment |
| **Liquidity** | Can be bought/sold on an exchange during market hours | Can take weeks or months to sell |
| **Management** | Professionally managed, no landlord responsibilities | Requires active management (or hiring a property manager) |
| **Diversification** | Easy to hold many properties across many locations via one investment | Concentrated in whatever specific properties you own |
| **Dividend income** | Required to distribute most taxable income as dividends | Rental income depends entirely on your own property's occupancy |

## Why some investors include REITs in a portfolio

- **Diversification**: Real estate doesn't always move in the same direction as stocks and bonds, so REITs can offer diversification benefits within a broader portfolio, though they aren't immune to market-wide downturns.
- **Income**: The dividend requirement makes REITs a common choice for investors seeking regular income, similar to the reasoning covered in our [dividends guide](/blog/what-are-dividends/).
- **Accessibility**: REITs let investors with modest amounts of money gain exposure to large-scale commercial real estate that would otherwise be completely out of reach.

## Risks to understand

- **Interest rate sensitivity**: REITs often rely on borrowed money to finance properties, making them sensitive to changes in interest rates — rising rates can increase borrowing costs and sometimes pressure REIT share prices.
- **Sector-specific risk**: A REIT concentrated in one property type (like office space) can be significantly affected by trends specific to that sector, such as shifts toward remote work reducing demand for office space.
- **Not risk-free income**: While REITs are known for dividends, those dividends aren't guaranteed and can be reduced if the underlying properties underperform.
- **Taxation of dividends**: REIT dividends are often taxed differently than qualified stock dividends, sometimes at a higher rate in a standard taxable account — worth understanding or discussing with a tax professional, particularly for larger holdings.

## How to invest in REITs

Most investors gain REIT exposure through:
- **Individual publicly traded REITs**, bought like any other stock through a [brokerage account](/blog/what-is-a-brokerage-account/).
- **REIT-focused ETFs or mutual funds**, which hold a diversified basket of many REITs across different property types, reducing single-company risk — similar in principle to the diversification benefits covered in our [index funds guide](/blog/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/).

Non-traded REITs also exist, but they come with less liquidity and less transparency than publicly traded options, and generally require more caution and due diligence.

## Key takeaways

- REITs let you invest in real estate through the stock market, without directly owning or managing property.
- They're required to distribute most of their taxable income as dividends, making them a common income-focused investment.
- REITs carry their own risks, including interest rate sensitivity and sector-specific downturns.
- A REIT-focused ETF offers diversified real estate exposure without needing to research individual REIT companies.
