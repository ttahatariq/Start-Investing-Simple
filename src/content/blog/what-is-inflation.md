Inflation gets mentioned constantly in the news, usually attached to a percentage and a sense of alarm. But the concept itself is simpler than the headlines make it feel, and understanding it explains why "just keeping money in cash" is riskier than it sounds.

## What inflation actually means

Inflation is the rate at which prices for goods and services rise over time, which — from the other direction — means the purchasing power of a given amount of money falls over time. If inflation runs at 3% in a year, something that cost $100 at the start of the year would typically cost around $103 by the end of it.

This isn't a policy failure or a one-time event; it's a normal, ongoing feature of most economies, though the *rate* varies considerably over time.

## Why cash sitting still is quietly losing value

Here's the part that surprises people: money sitting in a low-interest (or zero-interest) account isn't "safe" from inflation just because the number on the statement never goes down. If inflation runs at 3% annually and your cash earns 0.1% interest, the real, inflation-adjusted value of that money is shrinking every year — you can simply buy less with it than you could before, even though the dollar figure looks unchanged.

This is a different kind of risk than a stock market crash — there's no dramatic drop you can point to on a chart, just a slow, quiet erosion that's easy to overlook precisely because nothing appears to be "happening."

## A simple illustration

Suppose you have $10,000 sitting in an account earning essentially no interest, and inflation averages 3% per year. After 10 years, that $10,000 still says "$10,000" on your statement — but its actual purchasing power has fallen to roughly $7,400 in today's terms. Nothing was stolen or lost in the conventional sense; it simply couldn't keep pace with rising prices.

## Why this matters for how you save and invest

This is a core reason financial guidance generally distinguishes between:

- **Money you'll need soon** (an [emergency fund](/blog/build-emergency-fund-before-investing/), a near-term goal): kept in cash or a [high-yield savings account](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/), prioritizing safety and access over growth, even though it may not fully outpace inflation.
- **Money you won't need for years** (retirement, long-term goals): generally invested in assets like stocks, which have historically outpaced inflation over long periods — though with more short-term volatility, as covered in our [market volatility guide](/blog/understanding-market-volatility/).

Leaving long-term money sitting entirely in cash "to be safe" can actually be its own form of risk — the risk of guaranteed, gradual loss of purchasing power, trading short-term stability for long-term erosion.

## How inflation relates to interest rates and returns

When evaluating any interest rate or investment return, it's worth thinking in terms of the **real return** — the return after subtracting inflation — rather than the **nominal return** (the raw stated number). A savings account paying 4% during a period of 5% inflation has a *negative* real return, even though the nominal number looks positive.

## Inflation isn't uniformly bad for everyone

Rising prices affect savers and borrowers differently — inflation reduces the real value of debt you owe just as it reduces the real value of cash you hold, which is part of why the topic is more nuanced than "inflation is universally bad." That said, for most people trying to build savings, protecting purchasing power over time is the more relevant concern.

## The bottom line

Inflation doesn't announce itself the way a market drop does, but it works continuously in the background, reducing what your money can actually buy over time. Understanding this is part of why "safe" cash and "growth-oriented" investing serve different purposes — cash protects against short-term uncertainty, while long-term investing aims to actually outpace the slow erosion inflation causes.
