**Most 401(k) fees are deducted quietly from your balance, so you never see a bill — but they can easily cost six figures over a career.** The biggest cost is usually your funds' **expense ratios**, followed by plan **administrative fees**. A well-run plan's index funds often cost under 0.20% a year; a total cost above about **1%** is a red flag worth checking.

Unlike a subscription or a utility bill, most people never see a line-item charge for their 401(k) fees — they're deducted quietly from the account itself, which is exactly why so few people know what they're actually paying, or whether it's reasonable.

## The main categories of 401(k) fees

**Investment fees (expense ratios).** As covered in more detail in our [expense ratios guide](/blog/understanding-expense-ratios/), each fund in your plan charges its own annual fee, expressed as a percentage of your balance. This is usually the largest cost category and the one you have the most control over, since it depends on which funds within your plan's menu you choose.

**Administrative fees.** These cover the cost of running the plan itself — recordkeeping, compliance, customer service. Some employers cover this cost entirely; others pass some or all of it on to participants, either as a flat fee or a percentage of assets.

**Individual service fees.** Charges for specific actions you personally take, like taking a [401(k) loan](/blog/401k-loans-and-hardship-withdrawals/) or requesting a specific type of distribution — these only apply if you use that specific service.

**Hidden or bundled costs.** Some funds include **12b-1 fees** (marketing and distribution charges) or **revenue sharing**, where part of a fund's expense ratio is used to pay the plan's administrator. These are included in the expense ratio but not always obvious.

## What's reasonable? A quick benchmark

| Fee type | Low | Typical | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index fund expense ratio | Under 0.10% | 0.10%–0.30% | Above 0.50% |
| Actively managed fund | Under 0.50% | 0.50%–1.00% | Above 1.00% |
| Target-date fund | Under 0.20% | 0.20%–0.70% | Above 0.75% |
| Total plan cost (all-in) | Under 0.50% | 0.50%–1.00% | Above 1.00% |

*General ranges only. Large employers' plans tend to be cheaper than small employers' plans because costs are spread over more participants.*

## Why a seemingly small fee difference matters

A 1% difference in annual fees might look trivial, but it compounds against you every year. Suppose you contribute **$10,000 a year for 30 years** and your investments earn 7% a year before fees:

| Annual fees | Net return | Approx. balance after 30 years | Cost vs. 0.25% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25% | 6.75% | ~$903,000 | — |
| 0.50% | 6.50% | ~$864,000 | ~$39,000 |
| 1.00% | 6.00% | ~$791,000 | ~$113,000 |
| 1.50% | 5.50% | ~$724,000 | ~$179,000 |

*Simplified illustration; actual results vary.*

Because these fees are automatically deducted rather than billed separately, this cost is easy to underestimate simply because you never have to consciously pay it.

## How to actually find your fees

Federal rules require plans to provide participants with fee information, typically through:

- **An annual fee disclosure document**, often sent automatically or available on your plan's website. Look for a table listing every fund with its expense ratio.
- **Your plan's fund fact sheets**, which list each fund's expense ratio individually.
- **Your quarterly account statement**, which must show any administrative or individual fees actually deducted from your account.

If you can't easily find this information, your HR or benefits department, or your plan's recordkeeper directly, should be able to provide it on request. FINRA's free Fund Analyzer tool can also help you compare fund costs.

## How to calculate what you're paying

Multiply your balance in each fund by that fund's expense ratio, then add any administrative fees from your statement:

- $40,000 in a target-date fund at 0.60% = **$240 a year**
- $10,000 in an S&P 500 index fund at 0.04% = **$4 a year**
- Plan administrative fee = **$60 a year**
- **Total: about $304 a year, or 0.61% of a $50,000 balance**

In this example, moving the target-date money into the index fund (or a cheaper target-date option, if one exists) could cut costs dramatically.

## What you can actually control

You generally can't change your plan's overall fee structure or administrative costs directly, but you usually can:

1. **Choose lower-cost funds** within your plan's available menu, when comparable options exist. Our guide to [choosing your 401(k) investments](/blog/how-to-choose-401k-investments/) shows how to compare them.
2. **Consolidate old accounts** via [rollover](/blog/401k-when-you-change-jobs/) if a previous employer's plan carries high fees relative to a current plan or IRA.
3. **Use an IRA for extra savings.** Once you've captured the full match, an IRA at a low-cost brokerage can be cheaper than a high-fee plan; see [Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA](/blog/roth-401k-vs-roth-ira/).
4. **Raise concerns with HR or plan committees** if fees seem unusually high relative to typical benchmarks — plan fiduciaries have a legal obligation to keep costs reasonable.

## Should you still contribute to a high-fee 401(k)?

Usually yes, at least up to the match. An employer match of 50% or 100% is an instant return that far outweighs even high fees. And Traditional contributions still give you a tax break. The calculation changes for money *beyond* the match, where a low-cost IRA may be the better home. If you work for a school or nonprofit, fees can be especially high in older annuity-based plans; see [403(b) vs. 401(k)](/blog/403b-vs-401k/).

## The bottom line

401(k) fees are easy to ignore precisely because they're invisible in day-to-day account activity, but they compound the same way returns do — quietly, and significantly, over decades. A few minutes reviewing your plan's fee disclosure and picking the lowest-cost funds available is one of the highest-leverage checks you can do on an account you'll likely hold for most of your working life.
