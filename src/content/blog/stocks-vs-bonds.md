Almost every investment portfolio, from a beginner's first brokerage account to a massive pension fund, is built around two fundamental building blocks: stocks and bonds. Understanding what each one actually represents — and how their risk and reward differ — is one of the most useful foundations you can build as a new investor.

## What is a stock?

A stock represents **partial ownership** in a company. When you buy a share of a company's stock, you become a part-owner (shareholder) of that business, no matter how small your stake is.

As a shareholder, you can benefit in two main ways:
- **Price appreciation**: if the company grows and becomes more valuable, the price of its stock may rise.
- **Dividends**: some companies distribute a portion of their profits to shareholders on a regular basis.

The flip side: if the company performs poorly, the value of your shares can fall — sometimes dramatically — and there's no guarantee of getting your original investment back.

## What is a bond?

A bond represents a **loan**. When you buy a bond, you're lending money to whoever issued it — a corporation, a city, or a national government — in exchange for their promise to pay you back the original amount (the "principal") at a set future date, plus periodic interest payments along the way (called the "coupon").

Unlike stocks, bonds don't represent ownership. You're a lender, not an owner, and your return is generally more predictable: you know the interest rate and the repayment date in advance, assuming the issuer doesn't default.

## Comparing risk and return

### Stocks: higher potential return, higher volatility
Historically, stocks have delivered higher average long-term returns than bonds, but with significantly more short-term ups and downs. A stock's price can swing by double-digit percentages in a matter of weeks based on company news, economic conditions, or broader market sentiment.

### Bonds: lower potential return, more stability
Bonds — particularly those issued by financially stable governments or large, creditworthy companies — tend to have more predictable, stable returns and smaller price swings. In exchange for that stability, their long-term average returns are typically lower than stocks.

## Why not just pick the "better" one?

It's tempting to think "stocks have higher returns, so why bother with bonds at all?" But bonds serve a different purpose than pure return-maximization:

- **Diversification.** Stocks and bonds often (though not always) respond differently to the same economic events, which can help smooth out a portfolio's overall ups and downs.
- **Reducing volatility as you approach a financial goal.** If you'll need your money soon — for retirement, a home purchase, a child's tuition — a portfolio that's too concentrated in stocks risks a poorly timed market downturn right when you need to withdraw. Bonds' relative stability can help cushion that risk.
- **Income.** Bonds can provide a more predictable stream of interest payments, which some investors — especially retirees — value for covering ongoing expenses.

## How the mix typically shifts over time

A common (though not universal) principle is that younger investors, with decades until they'll need their money, can typically afford to hold a higher percentage of stocks, since they have time to ride out short-term volatility. As someone gets closer to needing the money, gradually shifting toward a higher percentage of bonds can help reduce the risk of a bad market downturn derailing their plans right before they need to withdraw.

This is the logic behind "target-date funds," which automatically adjust their stock-to-bond ratio as a target year (like a retirement date) approaches.

## A simplified example

- A 25-year-old saving for retirement decades away might hold a portfolio that's mostly stocks, accepting more short-term volatility in exchange for higher expected long-term growth.
- A 60-year-old nearing retirement might hold a more balanced mix of stocks and bonds, prioritizing more stability since they'll need to start withdrawing soon.

The "right" mix depends heavily on individual circumstances — timeline, risk tolerance, and other income sources — so there's no single ratio that fits everyone.

## Key takeaways

- Stocks represent ownership in a company; bonds represent a loan to an issuer.
- Stocks generally offer higher potential long-term returns with more volatility; bonds generally offer more stability with lower potential returns.
- Most portfolios combine both, with the mix often shifting toward more bonds as a financial goal (like retirement) gets closer.
