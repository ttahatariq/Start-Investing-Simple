Many people know their salary as a single headline number, but that's rarely what actually arrives in their bank account. Understanding what happens between your gross salary and your take-home pay makes budgeting far more accurate and less confusing.

## Gross pay vs. net pay

- **Gross pay** is your total earnings before any deductions — the salary figure typically quoted in a job offer.
- **Net pay** (also called "take-home pay") is what actually deposits into your bank account, after all deductions are subtracted from your gross pay.

The gap between the two can be substantial, which is why budgeting based on your gross salary figure, rather than your actual net pay, is a common and understandable mistake for people newer to managing their own paycheck.

## Common deductions from your paycheck

### Taxes
- **Federal income tax**: Withheld based on your income level and the information you provided on your tax withholding form.
- **State and local income tax**: Applies depending on where you live and work; some states and localities have no income tax at all.
- **Payroll taxes** (Social Security and Medicare, in the U.S.): A fixed percentage generally withheld regardless of income level up to certain thresholds, funding programs like the [Social Security](/blog/social-security-basics/) system.

### Retirement contributions
If you contribute to a [401(k)](/blog/what-is-a-401k/) or similar employer-sponsored plan, your contribution is typically deducted directly from your paycheck — for a Traditional 401(k), this reduces your taxable income for the pay period; for a Roth 401(k), it doesn't reduce taxable income but grows tax-free instead, as covered in our [Traditional vs. Roth 401(k) guide](/blog/traditional-401k-vs-roth-401k/).

### Health insurance and benefits premiums
Your share of health, dental, or vision insurance premiums is often deducted directly from your paycheck, frequently on a pre-tax basis, which reduces your taxable income similarly to a Traditional retirement contribution.

### Other pre-tax benefits
Contributions to accounts like an [HSA](/blog/hsa-as-a-retirement-tool/) or a Flexible Spending Account (FSA), if your employer offers them, are also commonly deducted pre-tax directly from your paycheck.

### Other possible deductions
Depending on your circumstances, additional deductions might include wage garnishments, union dues, or voluntary deductions for things like life insurance or a workplace savings program.

## Why understanding this matters for budgeting

Building a budget — whether using the [50/30/20 rule](/blog/50-30-20-budget-rule/) or another [budgeting method](/blog/budgeting-methods-that-actually-work/) — should be based on your actual net pay, not your gross salary, since gross pay overstates the money you actually have available to allocate. A budget built around gross salary figures will consistently feel like it doesn't add up, simply because it's working from an inflated starting number.

## How to read your pay stub

Most pay stubs include:
- **Gross pay** for the period and year-to-date
- **A breakdown of each deduction category** (taxes, retirement, insurance, etc.)
- **Net pay** for the period and year-to-date

Reviewing this breakdown periodically — not just glancing at the final deposited amount — helps you understand exactly where your gross salary is going, and can help catch errors (an incorrect withholding election, a benefit deduction that shouldn't apply to you) that might otherwise go unnoticed.

## A note on adjusting your withholding

Your federal (and sometimes state) tax withholding is generally based on elections you make, often through a form completed when you're hired or updated later. Withholding too much results in a larger tax refund but means less money in each regular paycheck throughout the year; withholding too little can mean a smaller paycheck impact now but a potential tax bill (and possibly a penalty) later. Adjusting your withholding to more closely match your actual expected tax liability, rather than significantly over- or under-withholding, is generally a reasonable goal, though specifics depend on your individual tax situation.

## Key takeaways

- Gross pay is your total earnings before deductions; net pay is what actually reaches your bank account.
- Common deductions include taxes, retirement contributions, and insurance premiums — many of which reduce your taxable income when paid pre-tax.
- Budget based on your actual net pay, not your gross salary, to avoid a budget that consistently feels like it doesn't balance.
- Reviewing your pay stub periodically helps you understand and verify exactly where your gross pay is going.
