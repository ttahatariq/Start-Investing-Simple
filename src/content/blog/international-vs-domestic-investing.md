Many investors, especially in the U.S., build portfolios that are heavily or entirely concentrated in their home country's stock market. Whether that's a reasonable choice or a missed opportunity for diversification is a genuinely debated question in investing.

## What is "home country bias"?

Home country bias refers to the well-documented tendency for investors to disproportionately favor investments from their own country, well beyond what that country's share of the global economy might suggest. This happens across many countries, not just the U.S., and is generally attributed to familiarity, easier access to information, and simple habit, rather than a deliberate, reasoned decision to underweight international markets.

## The case for international diversification

- **Reduces concentration in a single economy.** Even a large, diversified domestic market represents one country's economic and political circumstances. International exposure spreads that risk across multiple economies, which don't always move in sync with each other.
- **Access to different growth opportunities.** Different regions and countries can experience different economic cycles and growth trends at different times — international exposure provides access to opportunities that a purely domestic portfolio would miss entirely.
- **Historical precedent for leadership rotation.** Historically, no single country's stock market has consistently outperformed all others over every extended period — market leadership has rotated among countries and regions over different multi-year periods.

## The case for staying more domestically focused

- **Simplicity.** A single, broad domestic [index fund](/blog/index-funds-vs-individual-stocks/) is easy to understand and manage, without needing to evaluate currency effects, differing tax treatment, or foreign market regulations.
- **Many domestic companies already have significant international exposure.** Many large domestic companies generate substantial revenue from international operations, which some argue already provides a degree of indirect global diversification, even within a domestic-only portfolio.
- **Historical performance in some periods.** Depending on the specific time period examined, a domestic-only portfolio has sometimes outperformed a globally diversified one — though the reverse has also been true in other periods, underscoring that this can't be reliably predicted in advance.

## Currency risk: an added factor with international investing

When you invest internationally, you're also exposed to currency fluctuations — even if a foreign company's stock performs well in its local currency, your actual return (converted back to your home currency) can be affected by how that currency moves relative to your own. This cuts both ways: currency movements can enhance or reduce your returns, adding an additional layer of variability beyond the underlying investment performance itself.

## A middle-ground approach

Many financial professionals suggest that a reasonable, moderate approach is to hold a meaningful allocation to international investments — often via a broad international index fund or ETF — alongside a domestic core holding, rather than choosing one exclusively over the other. The exact percentage allocated internationally is a matter of ongoing debate and personal preference, with no single universally agreed-upon "correct" answer, but the underlying diversification logic (not concentrating entirely in one country) applies to most reasonable portfolio approaches.

## How to add international exposure

- **International index funds or ETFs**: Broad funds covering developed international markets, emerging markets, or a combination of both, offering diversified exposure in a single fund — similar in structure to the [ETFs](/blog/what-is-an-etf/) covered in our earlier guide.
- **Total world index funds**: Some funds combine domestic and international exposure into a single fund weighted by global market size, offering broad diversification without needing to manage separate domestic and international allocations yourself.

## Key takeaways

- Home country bias is common but not necessarily optimal — it reflects familiarity more than a deliberate diversification strategy.
- International diversification spreads risk across multiple economies, though it introduces currency risk as an additional variable.
- No single approach (all-domestic, or heavily international) has consistently outperformed across all historical periods.
- A blended approach, combining domestic and international exposure, is a reasonable way to capture diversification benefits without needing to predict which region will perform best.
