**Vesting is the schedule that decides when your employer's 401(k) contributions legally become yours.** Your own contributions are always 100% yours, immediately. But matching and other employer contributions often vest over several years. Leave before you're fully vested, and you can forfeit part or all of that "free money."

Getting a [401(k) employer match](/blog/what-is-a-401k/) is often described as free money, and for the most part it is — but there's a catch many people don't discover until they leave a job earlier than expected. That catch is called vesting.

## What does "vested" actually mean?

Being vested in your employer's contributions means you've earned full legal ownership of that money. Until you're vested, leaving your job can mean forfeiting some or all of the employer contributions sitting in your account — even though the balance shows up on your statement.

Vesting applies to:

- **Employer matching contributions**
- **Employer profit-sharing or non-elective contributions**
- **Investment gains** on those employer contributions

It never applies to your own salary deferrals (Traditional or Roth) or the growth on them. Those are always 100% yours.

## The common types of vesting schedules

### Immediate vesting

Employer contributions are yours from day one. Less common than the alternatives, but it does exist at some employers, and it's required for certain "safe harbor" 401(k) contributions.

### Cliff vesting

You own 0% of employer contributions until a specific date, then jump to 100% all at once. By law, a cliff schedule for matching contributions can be **no longer than 3 years**. Leave one day before the cliff, and you walk away with none of the match; leave one day after, and it's all yours.

### Graded vesting

Ownership increases gradually over a set number of years. By law, a graded schedule can be **no slower than 6 years**, starting at 20% after year 2. Leaving partway through means keeping whatever percentage you'd already earned.

## Vesting schedules compared

| Years of service | Immediate | 3-year cliff (legal max) | 6-year graded (legal max) | Common 5-year graded |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Less than 1 | 100% | 0% | 0% | 0% or 20% |
| 1 | 100% | 0% | 0% | 20% |
| 2 | 100% | 0% | 20% | 40% |
| 3 | 100% | 100% | 40% | 60% |
| 4 | 100% | 100% | 60% | 80% |
| 5 | 100% | 100% | 80% | 100% |
| 6 | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |

A "year of service" is usually a plan year in which you worked at least 1,000 hours, but plans define it differently. Your plan's documents spell out exactly how it's counted.

## When you become 100% vested automatically

Regardless of your schedule, you generally become fully vested when:

- You reach the plan's **normal retirement age**
- The plan is **terminated** by your employer
- A **partial plan termination** occurs (typically when a large share of participants, often 20% or more, is let go in a given period)
- Many plans also fully vest you on **death or disability**

## Why this matters for job decisions

If you're considering leaving a job and you're close to a vesting milestone, it's worth checking your plan's specific schedule before deciding on a timeline. Walking away three weeks before a cliff-vesting date can mean forfeiting years of accumulated employer contributions — money that would have been fully yours with a short delay.

This isn't a reason to stay somewhere you're unhappy, but it is a concrete number worth knowing before making a final call, especially if a new opportunity's start date has any flexibility. You can also use it in negotiations: some new employers will offer a signing bonus to make up for a match you'd forfeit.

## A quick example

Suppose your employer uses a 5-year graded schedule (20% per year) and has contributed $8,000 in matching funds by your third year. At 60% vested (3 years × 20%), you'd be entitled to keep $4,800 of that if you left today — the remaining $3,200 would be forfeited back to the plan.

If you waited until your fifth anniversary, and your employer had contributed $14,000 by then, you'd keep all $14,000.

## What happens to your vested money when you leave?

Your own contributions plus the vested part of your employer's contributions are yours to keep. You can usually leave the money in the old plan, roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Our guide to [what happens to your 401(k) when you change jobs](/blog/401k-when-you-change-jobs/) covers each option. If you have an outstanding [401(k) loan](/blog/401k-loans-and-hardship-withdrawals/), check the repayment rules before you go.

## Where to find your vesting schedule

Your plan's Summary Plan Description (a document your employer or plan administrator is required to provide) will spell out the exact vesting schedule that applies. Many plan dashboards also show your current vested percentage and vested balance directly, updated as you accrue service time.

## Key things to remember

- Your own contributions are **always 100% vested**; vesting only affects employer money.
- Matching contributions must vest within **3 years (cliff)** or **6 years (graded)** at most.
- Check your vested balance before you set a last day, because a few weeks can be worth thousands.
- Vesting is a reason to time a move well, not to stay in a job that's wrong for you.
