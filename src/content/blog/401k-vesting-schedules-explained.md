Getting a [401(k) employer match](/blog/what-is-a-401k/) is often described as free money, and for the most part it is — but there's a catch many people don't discover until they leave a job earlier than expected. That catch is called vesting.

Your own contributions to a 401(k) are always 100% yours, immediately, no matter what. Vesting only applies to money your *employer* contributes on your behalf.

## What does "vested" actually mean?

Being vested in your employer's contributions means you've earned full legal ownership of that money. Until you're vested, leaving your job can mean forfeiting some or all of the employer contributions sitting in your account — even though the balance shows up on your statement.

## The common types of vesting schedules

**Immediate vesting.** Employer contributions are yours from day one. Less common than the alternatives, but it does exist at some employers.

**Cliff vesting.** You own 0% of employer contributions until a specific date (commonly around the two or three year mark), at which point you jump to 100% all at once. Leave one day before the cliff, and you walk away with none of the match; leave one day after, and it's all yours.

**Graded vesting.** Ownership increases gradually over a set number of years — for example, 20% per year over five years, reaching 100% at year five. Leaving partway through means keeping whatever percentage you'd already earned.

## Why this matters for job decisions

If you're considering leaving a job and you're close to a vesting milestone, it's worth checking your plan's specific schedule before deciding on a timeline. Walking away three weeks before a cliff-vesting date can mean forfeiting years of accumulated employer contributions — money that would have been fully yours with a short delay.

This isn't a reason to stay somewhere you're unhappy, but it is a concrete number worth knowing before making a final call, especially if a new opportunity's start date has any flexibility.

## Where to find your vesting schedule

Your plan's Summary Plan Description (a document your employer or plan administrator is required to provide) will spell out the exact vesting schedule that applies. Many plan dashboards also show your current vested percentage directly, updated as you accrue service time.

## A quick example

Suppose your employer uses a 5-year graded schedule (20% per year) and has contributed $8,000 in matching funds by your third year. At 60% vested (3 years × 20%), you'd be entitled to keep $4,800 of that if you left today — the remaining $3,200 would be forfeited back to the plan.

## Key things to remember

Vesting schedules are set by the employer within legal limits, not something you can negotiate mid-employment, but they're worth factoring into decisions about when to leave a job. Your own contributions are never at risk — this only ever affects the employer's side of the match.
