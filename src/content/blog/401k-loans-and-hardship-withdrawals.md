**A 401(k) loan lets you borrow from your own retirement savings and pay yourself back with interest. A hardship withdrawal lets you take money out permanently for a qualifying emergency, but you'll usually owe income tax and possibly a 10% penalty.** Both are safety valves, not routine tools: each one reduces the money working toward your retirement.

Retirement accounts are meant to stay invested for decades, but plans generally include mechanisms for accessing that money early in genuinely difficult situations. Here's how each one works, what it really costs, and what to consider first.

## 401(k) loans

Many (not all) plans allow you to borrow against your own vested 401(k) balance and repay it — with interest — back into your own account, usually through payroll deduction.

### How much you can borrow

The legal limit is generally the **lesser of $50,000 or 50% of your vested balance**. Some plans allow up to $10,000 even if that's more than half your balance. If you've had another loan in the past 12 months, the $50,000 cap is reduced by your highest outstanding balance during that time.

### Repayment rules

- Loans generally must be repaid within **5 years**, with payments at least quarterly (most plans deduct them from every paycheck).
- Loans used to buy your **primary home** can have a longer repayment period, if the plan allows.
- The interest rate is set by the plan, often the prime rate plus 1% to 2%.

**What makes it different from a bank loan:** you're borrowing from yourself, and the interest you pay goes back into your own account rather than to a lender. There's no credit check, and it doesn't appear on your credit report.

### The real costs

- **Lost growth.** While the loan is outstanding, that money isn't invested in the market. If your investments would have returned more than the loan's interest rate, you've given up that difference.
- **Reduced contributions.** Many people lower or stop their regular contributions while repaying a loan, missing out on future growth and sometimes the employer match.
- **Interest is paid with after-tax money.** In a Traditional 401(k), the interest you repay goes in after tax and will be taxed again when withdrawn in retirement.

### The biggest risk: leaving your job

If you leave your job (voluntarily or not) with a loan outstanding, many plans require the remaining balance to be repaid quickly. Under current rules you generally have until your **tax filing deadline (including extensions) for the year you left** to repay it or roll the amount into an IRA. If you don't, the outstanding balance is treated as a taxable distribution, plus a 10% penalty if you're under 59½. See [what happens to your 401(k) when you change jobs](/blog/401k-when-you-change-jobs/).

## Hardship withdrawals

A hardship withdrawal lets you take money directly from your 401(k) for an "immediate and heavy financial need," without needing to repay it. Unlike a loan, though, it permanently removes that money (and its future growth) from your retirement savings.

### What counts as a hardship

Most plans use the IRS "safe harbor" list of qualifying reasons:

- Unreimbursed **medical expenses** for you, your spouse, or dependents
- Costs to **buy your principal residence** (not mortgage payments)
- **Tuition and education fees** for the next 12 months
- Payments to **prevent eviction or foreclosure** on your main home
- **Funeral expenses**
- Certain **repairs to your home** after a casualty loss
- Expenses from a **federally declared disaster**

You can only withdraw the amount you need (which can include the taxes the withdrawal will trigger), and plans may let you self-certify that you have no other way to cover it.

### Taxes and penalties

Hardship withdrawals from a Traditional 401(k) are taxed as **ordinary income**. If you're under 59½, you'll generally also owe a **10% early withdrawal penalty** unless an exception applies (for example, certain large medical expenses). Hardship withdrawals can't be rolled back into the plan or an IRA.

Since 2020, plans can no longer suspend your contributions after a hardship withdrawal, and you're no longer required to take a loan first.

### Newer, smaller options

The SECURE 2.0 Act added a few narrower ways to access money penalty-free, if your plan offers them:

- **Emergency personal expense withdrawals** of up to $1,000 per year (you can repay it within three years)
- **Withdrawals for victims of domestic abuse**, up to a set limit
- **Qualified disaster withdrawals** for those affected by a federally declared disaster

Income tax still generally applies, but the 10% penalty doesn't.

## Comparing the two

| | 401(k) Loan | Hardship Withdrawal |
|---|---|---|
| **Repayment required** | Yes, usually with interest within 5 years | No |
| **Maximum amount** | Lesser of $50,000 or 50% of vested balance | Amount of the need (plus taxes) |
| **Reduces retirement balance permanently** | No, if repaid on schedule | Yes |
| **Income tax** | None, if repaid | Yes |
| **10% penalty under 59½** | Only if it defaults | Usually, unless an exception applies |
| **Credit check** | No | No |
| **Risk if you leave your job** | Balance may become due quickly | Not applicable |

## A quick example

Sam, 35, needs $10,000 for an emergency. He has $40,000 in his 401(k).

- **Loan:** He borrows $10,000 at 9% and repays about $208 a month for five years. No taxes, no penalty, and the interest goes back into his account. But if he's laid off in year two, he'll need to repay the roughly $6,500 balance by his tax deadline or owe taxes and a penalty on it.
- **Hardship withdrawal:** To net $10,000 in the 22% bracket, with the 10% penalty, he'd need to withdraw roughly $14,700, and that money never goes back into his retirement savings.

## Alternatives to consider first

Both options are generally considered after exhausting less costly alternatives:

1. **Your emergency fund.** This is exactly what it's for; see [why to build an emergency fund before investing](/blog/build-emergency-fund-before-investing/).
2. **Roth IRA contributions.** Your original Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty; see [Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA](/blog/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira/).
3. **Negotiating with the creditor.** Hospitals, landlords, and lenders often offer payment plans; see [how to negotiate bills](/blog/how-to-negotiate-bills/).
4. **A low-interest personal loan**, if your credit is good.

High-interest options like payday loans or carrying a credit card balance are usually worse than a 401(k) loan; see [the true cost of minimum payments](/blog/true-cost-of-minimum-payments/).

## When these might make sense

In a genuine emergency with no better alternative available, accessing your own retirement savings can be preferable to high-cost debt or losing your housing. A loan is usually the better of the two if your job is stable and you can afford the repayments. A hardship withdrawal is a last resort.

## The bottom line

401(k) loans and hardship withdrawals exist as safety valves, not as routine financial tools. A loan is repayable but risky if you change jobs; a hardship withdrawal is permanent and usually taxed and penalized. Understanding the difference matters before deciding either is the right move.
