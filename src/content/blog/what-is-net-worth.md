Income gets most of the attention in everyday conversation, but it's an incomplete picture of financial health. Net worth — a less commonly tracked number — often tells a more honest story about where you actually stand.

## What is net worth?

Net worth is the total value of everything you own (your assets) minus everything you owe (your liabilities).

Net worth = Total Assets − Total Liabilities

## What counts as an asset

- Cash in checking and savings accounts
- Investment and retirement account balances
- The current market value of a home, if you own one
- Vehicles (at their current resale value, not purchase price)
- Any other significant property you own

## What counts as a liability

- Mortgage balance
- Car loans
- Credit card balances
- Student loans
- Any other outstanding debt

## Why net worth matters more than income alone

Two people earning identical salaries can have wildly different net worths depending on their spending, saving, and debt habits — as discussed in our [lifestyle inflation guide](/blog/lifestyle-inflation/), high income doesn't automatically translate into financial progress if spending rises to match it.

Net worth captures the *result* of your financial decisions over time — saving, investing, paying down debt — in a single number, rather than just the *input* (income) those decisions were made from.

## A simple example

Suppose someone has $15,000 in savings and investments, a car worth $8,000, and no other significant assets — but owes $20,000 in student loans and $3,000 on a credit card.

Assets: $15,000 + $8,000 = $23,000
Liabilities: $20,000 + $3,000 = $23,000
Net worth: $0

This person could have a solid income and no visible financial "problems" day to day, while their net worth tells a more complete story: debt has fully offset what they've built so far. That's not a failure — it's useful information about where to focus.

## Can net worth be negative?

Yes, and it's more common than people assume, especially earlier in adulthood — student loans, a recent home purchase, or early-career lower income relative to debt can all put someone's net worth below zero temporarily. A negative net worth isn't inherently alarming on its own; what matters more is the direction it's moving.

## How often should you calculate it?

There's no required frequency, but many people find checking net worth once every few months, or once a year, strikes a reasonable balance — frequent enough to notice real trends, infrequent enough to avoid obsessing over short-term market swings in investment account balances.

## What net worth doesn't capture

- **Cash flow and income stability** — someone with a high net worth but no current income (early retirement, for example) faces different practical constraints than the number alone suggests.
- **Future earning potential** — a graduate with student debt and a low net worth may have very different prospects than someone with the same number and no further earning growth ahead.
- **How liquid your assets actually are** — a high net worth tied up mostly in home equity behaves very differently day to day than the same number held in a savings account.

## The bottom line

Net worth is a single, useful snapshot of your overall financial position — what you own minus what you owe — that captures the cumulative effect of your financial habits better than income alone. Tracking it periodically, and watching the trend rather than fixating on any single number, is one of the simplest ways to see whether your finances are actually moving in the direction you want.
