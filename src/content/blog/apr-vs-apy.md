**APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing money, and it generally ignores compounding. APY (annual percentage yield) is what you actually earn on savings in a year, including compounding.** Use **APR** to compare loans and credit cards, and **APY** to compare savings accounts and CDs. For the same rate, APY is always equal to or higher than APR.

APR and APY are two of the most commonly confused terms in personal finance — understandably, since they're built from nearly the same letters and often appear in similar contexts. But they answer different questions, and mixing them up can lead to real misunderstandings about what you're actually earning or paying.

## APR vs. APY at a glance

| | APR | APY |
|---|---|---|
| Stands for | Annual percentage rate | Annual percentage yield |
| Used for | Loans, credit cards, mortgages | Savings accounts, CDs, money market accounts |
| Includes compounding? | No | Yes |
| May include fees? | Yes (e.g., mortgage closing costs) | No |
| You want it to be | Lower | Higher |
| Required disclosure | Truth in Lending Act | Truth in Savings Act |

## APR: Annual Percentage Rate

APR represents the yearly cost of borrowing money, typically used for loans, credit cards, and mortgages. It's meant to reflect the interest rate plus certain fees, expressed as a yearly rate — though critically, **APR generally does not account for compounding** within the year.

- **Mortgages and auto loans:** APR includes the interest rate *plus* certain fees, such as origination fees and points. That's why a mortgage's APR is usually higher than its advertised interest rate.
- **Credit cards:** APR is essentially the interest rate. Cards often have several APRs: purchase APR, cash advance APR, penalty APR, and promotional 0% APRs.
- **Fixed vs. variable:** A variable APR, common on credit cards, moves with a benchmark like the prime rate.

## APY: Annual Percentage Yield

APY represents the actual amount you'll earn on a deposit (like a savings account) over a year, **including the effect of compounding** — interest earned on interest, as covered in more detail in our [compound interest guide](/blog/what-is-compound-interest/).

## The formula

You can convert a nominal rate (APR) into APY with:

**APY = (1 + r ÷ n)<sup>n</sup> − 1**

where **r** is the annual rate as a decimal and **n** is the number of compounding periods per year.

Here's how compounding frequency changes the APY on a 5% nominal rate:

| Compounding | Periods per year | APY |
|---|---|---|
| Annually | 1 | 5.00% |
| Quarterly | 4 | 5.09% |
| Monthly | 12 | 5.12% |
| Daily | 365 | 5.13% |

The more often interest compounds, the higher the APY, though the gains shrink as compounding gets more frequent.

## Why the distinction matters

Because APY accounts for compounding and APR doesn't, a savings account advertising a 5% APY isn't directly comparable to a loan advertising a 5% APR — they're measuring different things, even though the numbers look identical.

This is exactly why banks are required to advertise deposit account rates as APY rather than a simple annual rate — it's the more complete, accurate figure for savers.

## Example 1: a savings account

Suppose a savings account offers a 5% nominal annual rate, compounded monthly. Because of monthly compounding, the actual APY works out to roughly 5.12%. On $10,000, that's the difference between an expected $500 and the *actual* result of about $512 after one year, purely from the compounding effect. That's why a [high-yield savings account](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/) advertises its APY.

## Example 2: a credit card

Credit cards usually compound interest **daily**. A card with a 24% APR therefore costs more than 24% a year if you carry a balance:

(1 + 0.24 ÷ 365)<sup>365</sup> − 1 ≈ **27.1%**

On a $5,000 balance carried all year, that's roughly $1,356 in interest rather than the $1,200 the APR alone suggests. It's one reason carrying a balance is so expensive; see [the true cost of minimum payments](/blog/true-cost-of-minimum-payments/).

## Why lenders use APR instead

For loans, using APR (rather than an APY-style figure) is partly a matter of convention and regulation — it's the standard figure lenders are required to disclose, meant to give borrowers a way to compare loan costs across lenders on a similar basis. Some loans do compound in ways that make the *effective* cost slightly different from the stated APR, which is worth being aware of, particularly for revolving credit like credit cards.

## How to use this when comparing offers

- **Comparing savings accounts or CDs?** Compare APY figures directly — that's exactly what they're designed for.
- **Comparing loans or credit cards?** Compare APR figures, but also check for additional fees not captured in the headline rate, and how often interest actually compounds if you're carrying a balance.
- **Comparing mortgages?** Look at both the interest rate (your monthly payment) and the APR (the total cost including fees).
- **Watch promotional rates.** A 0% intro APR or a high "teaser" APY usually reverts after a set period.
- **Don't assume a lower nominal rate always means a lower cost or higher return** — the compounding frequency and fees behind the number can matter more than the headline figure suggests.

## What about investments?

You won't usually see APR or APY on stocks or funds. Investment returns vary from year to year, so they're quoted as historical or average annual returns, which aren't guaranteed. Our guide to [investments for beginners](/blog/investments-for-beginners/) explains the difference between guaranteed savings yields and market returns.

## The bottom line

APR and APY look almost identical on paper but represent genuinely different calculations — APR for the cost of borrowing, APY for the yield on savings, with APY specifically built to reflect compounding. When you borrow, look for the lowest APR; when you save, look for the highest APY. Knowing which figure you're looking at, and what it does and doesn't include, avoids a common source of confusion in comparing financial products.
