"Recession" is one of those words that shows up constantly in headlines, often well before — or well after — economists actually agree one has happened. Understanding what the term actually means helps cut through a lot of the noise.

## What officially defines a recession?

In the U.S., a recession is formally determined by a committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which looks at a broad range of economic indicators — employment, income, industrial production, and spending — rather than a single, simple rule.

A commonly cited informal shorthand is "two consecutive quarters of declining GDP" — and while this rough guideline lines up with many past recessions, it isn't the official definition, and the NBER's actual determination can sometimes differ from what this simple rule alone would suggest.

## Why recessions are often identified after they've started

Because the official determination relies on reviewing data across many months, recessions are frequently confirmed well after they've actually begun — sometimes many months later. This means by the time a recession is "officially" announced, the economy may already be partway through it, or in some cases, already recovering.

## What typically happens during a recession

- **Rising unemployment**, as businesses reduce spending and hiring.
- **Reduced consumer spending**, as households become more cautious.
- **Declining business investment and production.**
- **Often (though not always) declining stock prices**, reflecting reduced expectations for corporate profits — though markets frequently move in anticipation of a recession, sometimes before or after the actual economic data confirms one.

## How recessions relate to bear markets

As covered in our [bull vs. bear markets guide](/blog/bull-markets-vs-bear-markets/), a bear market (a significant stock market decline) often coincides with or precedes a recession, but the two aren't identical, and their timing doesn't always line up neatly. Markets sometimes decline in anticipation of an expected recession, and sometimes begin recovering before the broader economy has visibly turned around.

## How long-term investors should think about recessions

- **Recessions are a normal, recurring part of economic cycles**, not a sign that something has permanently gone wrong. Historically, economies and markets have recovered from every past recession, though this isn't a guarantee for any future one.
- **Trying to sell before a recession and buy back in after** requires accurately predicting timing that's genuinely difficult to get right consistently — the same challenge covered in our [market volatility guide](/blog/understanding-market-volatility/).
- **A properly matched [asset allocation](/blog/risk-tolerance-and-asset-allocation/)** for your actual timeline and risk tolerance is generally a more reliable approach than trying to react to recession headlines in real time.
- **Job security concerns during a recession are a genuinely different consideration than investment strategy** — this is part of why having an [emergency fund](/blog/build-emergency-fund-before-investing/) matters, so a job loss during an economic downturn doesn't force you to sell investments at a bad time.

## Recession fears vs. actual recessions

Financial media frequently discusses "recession fears" or "recession risk" well before — and sometimes without — an actual recession occurring. This kind of speculative discussion is common and doesn't necessarily predict what will actually happen; treating every recession discussion in the news as an imminent certainty can lead to unnecessary anxiety or premature financial decisions.

## The bottom line

A recession is a specific, retrospectively-determined economic designation, not simply "when the market drops" or "when the news gets negative." For long-term investors, recessions are a recurring, historically temporary feature of economic cycles — best approached with a well-matched asset allocation and financial cushion, rather than an attempt to predict and time around them.
