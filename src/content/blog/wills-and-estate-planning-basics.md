Estate planning has an image problem — it sounds like something only relevant to the wealthy or the elderly. In reality, a basic will and a couple of related documents matter for far more people, far earlier, than that image suggests.

## What is a will?

A will is a legal document specifying how you want your assets distributed after your death, and who you want to serve as executor (the person responsible for carrying out those wishes). Without one, decisions about your assets are instead made according to your state's default inheritance laws — which may not match what you would have actually wanted.

## Who actually needs a will?

More people than assume they do:

- **Anyone with dependents** — a will (often alongside a separate guardianship designation) specifies who would care for minor children, one of the most important reasons to have one regardless of how much you own.
- **Anyone with any assets at all** — a bank account, a car, personal belongings with sentimental or financial value — not just significant wealth.
- **Anyone who wants a say in what happens**, rather than leaving it entirely to default state law, which applies automatically if you die without a will (referred to as dying "intestate").

## What happens if you don't have a will

Each state has its own default intestacy laws determining how assets are distributed without a will — typically following a set order among spouses, children, and other relatives. This default order may not match your actual wishes, and the process can take longer and cost more (through probate court) than a well-organized estate plan would have required.

## Beyond a basic will: other documents worth knowing about

- **Beneficiary designations.** Retirement accounts (like a [401(k)](/blog/what-is-a-401k/) or IRA) and life insurance policies typically pass directly to named beneficiaries, regardless of what your will says — meaning these designations need to be kept up to date separately, and mismatches between your will and your beneficiary forms can cause real confusion.
- **A power of attorney.** A document authorizing someone to make financial decisions on your behalf if you become unable to do so yourself.
- **A healthcare directive (or living will).** Specifies your wishes for medical treatment if you're unable to communicate them yourself, and can designate someone to make healthcare decisions on your behalf.

## Common misconceptions

**"I don't have enough to need a will."** Even modest assets, or simply having strong preferences about who should raise your children, are reasons enough — a will isn't only for large estates.

**"I'm too young for this to matter."** Accidents and unexpected medical events aren't limited to older adults, and beneficiary designations in particular matter from the moment you open a retirement account, regardless of age.

**"A will covers everything automatically."** As noted above, retirement accounts and life insurance generally pass via beneficiary designations, not your will — a will alone doesn't override those.

## How to actually get started

1. **List your assets** and think through who you'd want to receive them.
2. **Decide on an executor** — someone you trust to carry out your wishes.
2. **Consider guardianship designations** if you have minor children.
3. **Check and update beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts and insurance policies, separately from your will.
4. **Use an estate planning attorney for anything beyond the simplest situations** — online will-making tools exist for straightforward cases, but complex family situations, business ownership, or significant assets generally benefit from professional legal guidance.

## The bottom line

Estate planning isn't reserved for the wealthy or the elderly — a basic will, updated beneficiary designations, and a couple of related documents matter for nearly everyone with any assets or dependents at all. The main cost of skipping it isn't necessarily financial; it's leaving decisions about your own wishes to default laws that may not reflect what you'd actually want.
