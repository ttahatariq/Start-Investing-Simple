403(b) vs. 401(k): What's the Difference?

A 403(b) and a 401(k) share the same 2027 contribution limit ($25,500), but differ in who offers them, investment options, fees, and a special 15-year catch-up. Here's how they compare.

A 403(b) and a 401(k) are both employer retirement plans with the same contribution limit ($25,500 for 2027). The main difference is who offers them: 401(k)s come from private, for-profit companies, while 403(b)s come from public schools, colleges, hospitals, churches, and other nonprofits. Day to day they work almost identically. The differences that matter are investment options, fees, regulatory oversight, and a special catch-up rule for long-serving 403(b) participants.

If you’re new to workplace retirement plans in general, start with our guide to what a 401(k) is and how it works. Almost everything there applies to a 403(b) too.

What they have in common

A 403(b) works a lot like a 401(k) in the ways that matter most:

  • Payroll contributions. You choose a percentage of each paycheck, and it’s invested automatically.
  • Traditional and Roth options. Contributions can be made before tax (Traditional) or after tax with tax-free qualified withdrawals (Roth), mirroring the Traditional vs. Roth 401(k) choice.
  • Same contribution limits. Annual contribution limits are the same as 401(k) contribution limits ($25,500 for 2027), adjusted periodically.
  • Employer matching. Many employers offer a match that works the same way a 401(k) match does, sometimes with a vesting schedule.
  • Catch-up contributions. The same catch-up contribution rules apply at 50 and older, including the higher catch-up at ages 60–63.
  • Same withdrawal rules. Withdrawals before 59½ are generally subject to income tax and a 10% penalty, with exceptions. Many plans allow loans and hardship withdrawals.
  • Rollovers. When you leave a job, you can usually roll your balance into an IRA or a new employer’s plan, just like moving a 401(k) when you change jobs.

Where they differ

1. Who offers them

401(k)s are offered by private-sector, for-profit employers. 403(b)s are offered by public schools and universities, hospitals, churches, and tax-exempt 501(c)(3) organizations like charities and foundations. If you’re a teacher, nurse, professor, or nonprofit employee, there’s a good chance your plan is a 403(b).

2. Investment options

By law, 403(b) plans can generally invest only in annuity contracts and mutual funds (held in custodial accounts). Historically, many plans were dominated by annuities sold by insurance companies. Many modern plans now offer low-cost index mutual funds similar to a good 401(k) menu, but quality varies a lot from employer to employer.

401(k) plans have more flexibility and, especially at large employers, often include low-cost index funds, target-date funds, and sometimes a brokerage window.

3. Fees

This is where 403(b) participants most need to pay attention. Annuity-based options can carry layered costs: insurance charges, administrative fees, and surrender charges if you move money too soon. Some plans charge more than 1% a year in total, compared with under 0.10% for a simple index fund.

Over a 30-year career, that gap can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Check your plan’s fee disclosure and compare it with what you’d pay in a 401(k) (see what you’re actually paying in 401(k) fees), using the same due diligence covered in our guide on choosing 401(k) investments.

4. Regulatory oversight (ERISA)

Most 401(k)s are covered by ERISA, the federal law that sets fiduciary standards and disclosure requirements for retirement plans. Many 403(b) plans, including those run by public schools, state universities, and churches, are exempt. That doesn’t make them unsafe, but it can mean fewer protections around fees and investment choices, so it’s worth reading your plan documents more carefully.

5. The 15-year catch-up

Some 403(b) plans offer a special additional catch-up for employees with 15 or more years of service at the same qualifying organization (such as a school district, hospital, or church). It allows up to $3,000 extra per year, with a $15,000 lifetime cap, on top of the regular limit. It can be combined with the age-50 catch-up, though the rules on how the two interact are technical, so confirm the details with your plan administrator.

This provision has no equivalent in 401(k) plans, and not every 403(b) offers it.

What about a 457(b)?

A 457(b) is another employer-sponsored plan, common among state and local government employees. It has its own separate contribution limit, which means some employees have access to both a 403(b) and a 457(b) and can contribute up to the full limit in each, potentially doubling their tax-advantaged savings capacity.

Governmental 457(b) plans have another unusual perk: withdrawals after you leave that employer generally aren’t subject to the 10% early withdrawal penalty, even before 59½.

Can you have both a 401(k) and a 403(b)?

Yes. It’s common for people who work two jobs, change jobs mid-year, or switch between the private and nonprofit sectors. The key rule: your employee contributions across all 401(k) and 403(b) plans combined can’t exceed the annual limit ($25,500 for 2027, plus any catch-up). Employer contributions are tracked separately for each plan.

Each employer only knows what you contributed to their plan, so it’s up to you to stay under the combined limit.

A quick example

Priya is a 35-year-old hospital nurse earning $80,000. Her 403(b) matches 100% of contributions up to 4%.

  • She contributes 10% ($8,000), and her employer adds $3,200.
  • She reviews her plan’s fee disclosure, skips the annuity option with a 1.2% annual cost, and chooses a target-date index fund costing 0.12%.
  • If her employer also offered a 457(b), she could contribute to that too, on top of her 403(b).

Over 30 years at a 7% return before fees, $11,200 a year grows to roughly $1.06 million. After fees, choosing the 1.2% option instead of the 0.12% fund would leave her with roughly $180,000 less.

What to do if you have a 403(b)

The same core principles that apply to a 401(k) generally apply here:

  1. Contribute enough to get any available employer match — then use our guide on how much to contribute to your 401(k) or 403(b) to set a longer-term rate.
  2. Review the actual investment options and their fees — don’t assume they’re automatically comparable to a typical low-cost 401(k) lineup.
  3. Consider whether Traditional or Roth contributions (or a mix) make sense for your tax situation — and how a Roth 403(b) compares to a Roth IRA, covered in our Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA guide.
  4. If you’re 50+ or have long service at a qualifying employer, check whether additional catch-up provisions apply to you specifically.
  5. If you have access to a 457(b) as well, consider using both once your budget allows.

The bottom line

A 403(b) and a 401(k) serve the same basic purpose — tax-advantaged retirement savings through your employer — and share most of the same core mechanics, including the same contribution limits. The differences that matter most are who’s eligible, what investment options and fees your plan actually has, and a couple of catch-up provisions unique to 403(b) plans. Your specific plan’s quality matters far more than the label.

Frequently asked questions/

What is the main difference between a 403(b) and a 401(k)?+

The main difference is who offers them. A 401(k) is offered by private, for-profit employers, while a 403(b) is offered by public schools, colleges, hospitals, churches, and many other nonprofits. They share the same contribution limits and work the same way day to day, but 403(b) plans can have more limited investment options and sometimes higher fees.

Is a 403(b) better than a 401(k)?+

Neither is better by design. A 403(b) can offer a special 15-year catch-up for long-serving employees, but 401(k) plans often have broader, lower-cost investment menus. The quality of your specific plan, including its fees, fund options, and employer match, matters more than the plan type.

Can I contribute to both a 403(b) and a 401(k)?+

Yes, if you have access to both, but your employee contributions to all 401(k) and 403(b) plans combined can't exceed the annual limit, which is $25,500 for 2027 plus any catch-up you qualify for. A governmental 457(b) plan has its own separate limit.

Can I roll a 403(b) into a 401(k) or an IRA?+

Generally yes. When you leave your job, you can usually roll a 403(b) into a new employer's 401(k) (if the plan accepts rollovers) or into an IRA, without taxes or penalties if it's done as a direct rollover.

What is the 403(b) 15-year catch-up?+

Some 403(b) plans let employees with at least 15 years of service at a qualifying organization, such as a school, hospital, or church, contribute up to an extra $3,000 a year, with a $15,000 lifetime cap. It's separate from the age-50 catch-up, and not every plan offers it.
