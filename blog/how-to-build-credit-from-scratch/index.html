How to Build Credit From Scratch (No Credit History)

Practical steps for building a credit history when you're starting with none — secured cards, becoming an authorized user, and what to avoid.

Having no credit history creates an odd catch-22: many ways to build credit require already having some credit, and many lenders are hesitant to extend credit to someone with no track record at all. Fortunately, there are established, practical ways to break into the system from zero.

Why building credit matters

As covered in our credit scores guide, a strong credit history can lead to better interest rates on loans, easier approval for apartments and some utilities, and access to better credit card terms. Starting from scratch, the priority is simply establishing some track record for scoring models to evaluate.

Practical ways to start building credit

1. Become an authorized user on someone else’s card

If a family member or close friend has a credit card in good standing, being added as an authorized user can allow that account’s history to appear on your own credit report — potentially giving you a head start, since you benefit from their established payment history. This requires trust on both sides, since the primary cardholder remains fully responsible for the debt.

2. Get a secured credit card

A secured credit card requires a cash deposit (often equal to your credit limit) as collateral, making it accessible even without prior credit history, since the deposit reduces the lender’s risk. Used responsibly — small purchases, paid in full each month — a secured card can help establish a track record, and some issuers will “graduate” you to a standard unsecured card after a period of responsible use.

3. Apply for a student credit card, if eligible

Many issuers offer credit cards specifically designed for students, often with more lenient approval requirements than standard cards, recognizing that students are just beginning to build financial history.

4. Become a co-signer or joint account holder, cautiously

Similar to being an authorized user, but with more shared responsibility — a joint account or co-signed loan means you’re directly responsible for the debt, not just benefiting from someone else’s history. This carries more risk for both parties and should be approached carefully.

5. Consider a credit-builder loan

Some financial institutions offer small loans specifically designed to help build credit: the loan amount is held in a locked account while you make payments, and you receive access to the funds (plus, sometimes, the interest) once fully paid off — with the payment history reported to credit bureaus along the way.

What to do once you have your first credit account

  • Pay on time, every time. As covered in our credit scores guide, payment history is generally the single largest factor in most scoring models.
  • Keep your balance low relative to your limit. Using a small percentage of your available credit, even if you pay in full monthly, tends to be viewed favorably by scoring models.
  • Avoid applying for multiple new accounts in a short period, which can temporarily lower your score and may signal risk to lenders.
  • Be patient. Credit history takes time to build meaningfully — there’s no reliable shortcut to a long track record.

Mistakes to avoid when starting out

  • Carrying a balance to “build credit faster.” This is a common myth — carrying an unpaid balance doesn’t build credit any faster than paying in full, and it costs you unnecessary interest.
  • Opening too many accounts too quickly, which can look risky to lenders and complicate managing multiple due dates.
  • Missing payments early on, which can have an outsized negative effect on a still-thin credit history compared to an established one that can better absorb an occasional mistake.
  • Maxing out a secured or student card, even if you plan to pay it off — high utilization is generally viewed unfavorably regardless of your intent to pay it off soon after.

How long does it take to build meaningful credit?

There’s no fixed timeline, but building a reasonably established credit history often takes at least a year or more of consistent, responsible use, with continued improvement over several years as your track record lengthens. Starting early — even with small, deliberate steps — tends to pay off well before you actually need strong credit for a major purchase like a car or home.

Key takeaways

  • Secured credit cards, becoming an authorized user, and credit-builder loans are common, accessible ways to start building credit from zero.
  • Payment history and low credit utilization matter most once you have an account — the specific account type matters less than how responsibly you use it.
  • Avoid common myths, like believing you need to carry a balance to build credit.
  • Building meaningful credit history takes sustained time — there’s no reliable shortcut.
