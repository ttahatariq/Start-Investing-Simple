Investments for Beginners: 9 Simple Places to Start (and What to Skip)

The best investments for beginners are simple, low-cost, and diversified. Here are 9 to know — from 401(k)s and Roth IRAs to index funds and ETFs — plus the order to use them in.

The best investments for beginners are simple, low-cost, and diversified. For most people, that means broad-market index funds or ETFs, held inside a tax-advantaged account like a 401(k) or Roth IRA, and added to automatically every month. You don’t need to pick stocks, time the market, or have a lot of money to start.

This guide covers the nine investments and accounts worth knowing as a beginner, what each one is good for, and the order most people should use them in. Every section links to a deeper guide if you want more detail.

Before you invest: two quick checks

Investing works best when you won’t be forced to sell at a bad time. Before putting money into the market:

  1. Have a starter emergency fund. Even $1,000 to $2,000 in savings keeps a surprise bill from turning into a forced sale or new debt. Here’s why to build an emergency fund before investing.
  2. Deal with high-interest debt. Paying off a 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% “return.” See whether to pay off debt or invest first.

One exception: if your employer offers a 401(k) match, it’s usually worth capturing even while you work on the two steps above.

Part 1: Where to invest (the accounts)

Think of accounts as the containers and investments as what goes inside them. Picking the right container can save you thousands in taxes.

1. A 401(k) (or 403(b)) through your employer

A 401(k) lets you invest part of every paycheck before you see it, often with free matching money from your employer. For 2027, you can contribute up to $25,500.

  • Best for: anyone whose employer offers one, especially with a match
  • Start with: enough to get the full match
  • Learn more: What is a 401(k)? · How much should you contribute? · 403(b) vs. 401(k)

2. A Roth IRA (or Traditional IRA)

An IRA is a retirement account you open yourself. A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax money and grows completely tax-free, which is a great deal for younger investors who expect their income to rise. The limit is $7,500 for 2027.

  • Best for: beginners who want flexibility and full control over investment choices
  • Learn more: Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA · Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA

3. A regular brokerage account

A taxable brokerage account has no contribution limits and no withdrawal restrictions. In exchange, it has no special tax breaks. It’s where you invest for goals before retirement, or once your retirement accounts are full.

  • Best for: medium- to long-term goals, or extra savings beyond retirement accounts
  • Learn more: What is a brokerage account?

4. A high-yield savings account (for money you’ll need soon)

Not technically an investment, but it’s the right home for your emergency fund and anything you’ll spend within the next few years. It’s FDIC-insured and pays far more interest than a typical bank savings account.

  • Best for: emergency funds and short-term goals
  • Learn more: High-yield savings accounts explained

Part 2: What to invest in (the investments)

5. Index funds

An index fund holds every company in a market index, like the S&P 500 or the total U.S. stock market. One purchase gives you a slice of hundreds or thousands of companies, with fees often under 0.10% a year. For most beginners, this is the core of the portfolio.

  • Why beginners like them: instant diversification, very low cost, no stock-picking
  • Learn more: Index funds vs. individual stocks

6. ETFs (exchange-traded funds)

An ETF is a fund that trades on the stock exchange like a single stock. Many of the most popular index funds are ETFs. They’re easy to buy in any brokerage account or IRA, often with no minimum beyond the price of one share, or even a fraction of one.

  • Learn more: What is an ETF?

7. Bonds and bond funds

Bonds are loans to governments or companies that pay you interest. They usually grow more slowly than stocks but swing much less, which is why they’re added to portfolios to smooth out the ride, especially as you get closer to needing the money.

  • Learn more: Stocks vs. bonds · Risk tolerance and asset allocation

8. Target-date funds and robo-advisors (the “do it for me” options)

If choosing funds feels overwhelming, let someone else do it:

  • A target-date fund (e.g., a “2060 fund”) holds a mix of stock and bond index funds and automatically gets more conservative as its target year approaches. It’s a common default in 401(k) plans; see how to choose your 401(k) investments.
  • A robo-advisor builds and rebalances a portfolio for you based on a short questionnaire, for a small annual fee. See what a robo-advisor is.

9. Dividend stocks and REITs (once you have the basics)

Some companies pay you a share of their profits as dividends, and REITs let you invest in real estate without buying property. Both can play a role, but most beginners already own plenty of both through a total-market index fund.

  • Learn more: What are dividends? · What is a REIT?

Investments for beginners at a glance

Investment Risk level Typical cost Best for
High-yield savings Very low Free Emergency fund, short-term goals
Bond funds Low–medium Low Stability, nearer-term goals
Total-market index fund / ETF Medium–high (short term) Very low The core of a long-term portfolio
Target-date fund Varies by date Low–moderate Hands-off retirement investing
Robo-advisor Varies Low annual fee Hands-off investing outside work
Individual stocks High Low to trade A small “learning” slice, if any

The order to do it in

Here’s a simple, widely used sequence. Adjust it to your situation:

  1. Starter emergency fund in a high-yield savings account
  2. 401(k) up to the full employer match
  3. Pay off high-interest debt
  4. Roth IRA, invested in a low-cost index fund or target-date fund
  5. Raise your 401(k) contribution toward 15% of income (see the 2027 401(k) limits)
  6. Regular brokerage account for anything beyond that

Simple habits that matter more than what you pick

  • Keep costs low. A 1% fee can eat a surprising share of your returns over 30 years. See understanding expense ratios.
  • Invest automatically. Contributing a fixed amount on a schedule, known as dollar-cost averaging, removes the temptation to time the market.
  • Give it time. Growth builds on itself through compound interest. Our compound interest calculator shows how much time matters.
  • Expect drops. Markets fall regularly; understanding volatility makes it easier to stay invested when they do.
  • Know your risk tolerance. Take our risk tolerance quiz before choosing your stock-and-bond mix.

What beginners should skip (for now)

None of these are off-limits forever, but they’re easy ways to lose money before you understand the basics:

  • Day trading and frequent trading. Most active traders underperform a simple index fund.
  • Options and leveraged ETFs. Complex products that can lose value very quickly.
  • “Hot tips” and individual stocks with money you can’t afford to lose. If you want to pick stocks, keep it to a small slice of your portfolio.
  • Anything promising guaranteed high returns. That’s a classic warning sign of a scam.

A beginner example

Jordan is 25, earns $55,000, and has $2,000 in a high-yield savings account. Their employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6%.

  • 401(k): contributes 6% ($3,300/year) into a target-date fund and gets $1,650 in matching money.
  • Roth IRA: sets up an automatic $200/month ($2,400/year) into a total-market index ETF.
  • Total: $5,700 a year of their own money, plus $1,650 from their employer.

At a 7% average annual return, $7,350 a year for 40 years could grow to roughly $1.5 million. That’s not a guarantee, but it shows what simple, consistent investing can do.

Key takeaways

  • The best investments for beginners are low-cost, diversified index funds or ETFs.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts first: a 401(k) up to the match, then a Roth IRA.
  • Keep your emergency fund in a high-yield savings account, not the stock market.
  • If choosing feels overwhelming, a target-date fund or robo-advisor is a perfectly good choice.
  • Consistency beats cleverness: invest automatically, keep fees low, and give it time.

Frequently asked questions/

What are the best investments for beginners?+

For most beginners, the best investments are simple, low-cost, and diversified: broad-market index funds or ETFs, held inside tax-advantaged accounts like a 401(k) or Roth IRA. A target-date fund or a robo-advisor can handle the choices for you if you'd rather not pick funds yourself.

How much money do I need to start investing?+

Very little. Many brokerages have no account minimums and let you buy fractional shares of ETFs and stocks for as little as $1 to $5. A 401(k) lets you start with a small percentage of each paycheck.

Where should a beginner invest first?+

A common order is: build a starter emergency fund, contribute to your 401(k) up to the full employer match, pay off high-interest debt, fund a Roth IRA, then increase 401(k) contributions or invest in a regular brokerage account.

Are index funds good for beginners?+

Yes. Index funds are one of the most widely recommended investments for beginners because they spread your money across hundreds or thousands of companies, have very low fees, and require no stock-picking.

What investments should beginners avoid?+

Beginners are usually better off avoiding highly speculative or complex investments until they understand the basics: individual stock picking with money they can't afford to lose, options, leveraged ETFs, day trading, and anything promising guaranteed high returns.
