How Much Should I Contribute to My 401(k)? Benchmarks by Age and Income

Contribute at least enough to get your full employer match, then work toward 15% of income. Here are 401(k) contribution benchmarks by age, with examples and per-paycheck math.

Contribute at least enough to get your full employer match, then work toward 15% of your income, including the match. That’s the short answer to “how much should I contribute to my 401(k)?” If 15% isn’t realistic yet, start with what you can, even 3% to 6%, and raise it by 1% a year or every time you get a raise.

“How much should I be contributing to my 401(k) right now?” is one of the most common questions from people who have a 401(k) plan available but aren’t sure whether their current contribution rate is reasonable. There isn’t one universal answer, but there are useful benchmarks by age and income.

401(k) contribution benchmarks by age

Age Suggested contribution (incl. match) Focus
20s At least the full match, working toward 10%–15% Build the habit; time is your biggest advantage
30s 10%–15% Increase with every raise
40s 15% or more Make up for any gaps from earlier years
50s 15%–20%+ Use catch-up contributions if behind
60–63 As much as you can afford Use the larger “super” catch-up ($11,750 in 2027)

These are common reference points, not personalized targets. If you started later, you’ll generally need a higher rate to reach the same goal.

Early career: building the habit

At the start of a career, the priority is usually contributing enough to capture any employer match in full — often somewhere in the 3-6% range, though this varies by employer — while also building an emergency fund and managing any existing debt. A commonly cited longer-term target is working toward 10-15% of income (including any employer match) over time, but starting lower and increasing gradually is far more realistic than hitting that number immediately on a starting salary.

The good news: money you contribute in your 20s has 40+ years to grow. Even small amounts now can outgrow much larger contributions made later, thanks to compound interest.

Mid-career: increasing the rate

As income tends to rise through a career, mid-career is often a natural point to increase your contribution percentage — ideally directing at least a portion of each raise toward retirement savings rather than letting it fully disappear into lifestyle inflation. By this stage, many people aim to be contributing somewhere in the 15% range (including employer match), adjusted based on how much they saved earlier and what their retirement goals actually require.

Approaching retirement: catching up if needed

If contributions started later or were interrupted, the years approaching retirement are when catch-up contributions become available, allowing people 50 and older to contribute beyond the standard annual limit. This stage often also involves a closer look at actual retirement income needs, as covered in our how much do you need to retire guide, rather than relying purely on generic percentage targets.

How much difference does your rate make?

Here’s what different contribution rates could grow to for someone earning $60,000 whose employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, invested for 35 years at a 7% average annual return:

Your contribution You put in per year Employer adds Total per year Approx. value after 35 years
6% $3,600 $1,800 $5,400 ~$747,000
10% $6,000 $1,800 $7,800 ~$1.08 million
15% $9,000 $1,800 $10,800 ~$1.49 million

Simplified illustration: assumes no raises, a constant 7% return, and ignores inflation and fees. Real results will vary.

On this salary, each extra percentage point costs $600 a year before tax (less after the tax savings), yet moving from 6% to 10% could mean roughly $330,000 more at retirement. Run your own numbers with our Retirement Savings Calculator.

It costs less than you think per paycheck

With a Traditional 401(k), contributions come out before federal income tax, so your take-home pay drops by less than the amount you contribute. If you’re in the 22% federal tax bracket, a $100 contribution reduces your take-home pay by roughly $78 (state taxes can lower it further). With a Roth 401(k), there’s no upfront tax break, but withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Our guide to Traditional vs. Roth 401(k) explains how to choose.

To see where every dollar of your paycheck goes, read understanding your paycheck.

Three ways to increase your rate painlessly

  1. Turn on auto-escalation. Many plans can raise your contribution by 1% automatically every year. You’ll barely notice the difference.
  2. Save half of every raise. If you get a 4% raise, add 2% to your 401(k). Your take-home pay still goes up.
  3. Redirect finished payments. When you pay off a car loan or credit card, send part of that freed-up money to your 401(k).

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Not getting the full match. It’s an instant, guaranteed return. Missing it is like turning down part of your salary.
  • Forgetting to re-enroll after a job change. Your rate doesn’t carry over to a new employer, and default rates are often low (3% is common).
  • Never increasing your rate. Many people stay at their starting percentage for years.
  • Front-loading too fast. If you hit the annual limit early and your plan doesn’t do a year-end “true-up,” you can lose part of your match. See the 2027 401(k) contribution limits for per-paycheck amounts.
  • Cashing out when you leave a job. Taxes and penalties can wipe out a big chunk. Here’s what to do with your 401(k) when you change jobs.

Why generic benchmarks have real limits

These percentage ranges are common reference points, not personalized targets. They don’t account for:

  • Other retirement resources (a pension, other investments, a spouse’s savings)
  • Regional cost of living differences
  • How many years you actually have until you plan to retire
  • Whether you’re prioritizing other financial goals (debt payoff, a house down payment) at the same time

Two people the same age with the same salary can reasonably have very different “right” contribution rates depending on these factors. The same caveats apply to savings benchmarks by age.

A more useful approach than chasing a percentage

Rather than fixating on hitting a specific percentage because it appeared in an article (including this one), a more grounded approach is:

  1. Capture the full employer match, at minimum.
  2. Estimate your actual retirement number using a tool like our Retirement Savings Calculator.
  3. Work backward to figure out roughly what contribution rate, sustained over your remaining working years, gets you there.
  4. If your budget allows more, decide whether to go all the way to the IRS limit — see should you max out your 401(k)?
  5. Revisit that number periodically as your income, goals, and circumstances change.

The bottom line

Start with the full employer match, aim for 15% of income including the match, and raise your rate a little every year. Age-based benchmarks are a reasonable starting reference, not a scorecard to measure yourself against. What actually matters is whether your contribution rate is realistically moving you toward your own retirement goal.

Frequently asked questions/

How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?+

At a minimum, contribute enough to get your full employer match. Beyond that, a common long-term target is 15% of your pre-tax income, including the employer match. If that isn't possible yet, start lower and increase your rate by 1% each year or with each raise.

Is 10% a good 401(k) contribution?+

Yes, 10% is a solid contribution rate, especially early in your career. Combined with a typical employer match, it puts you close to the commonly recommended 15% total savings rate.

What percentage should I contribute to my 401(k) at 25?+

At 25, aim to contribute at least enough to get your full employer match, and ideally work toward 10% to 15% including the match. Starting early matters more than the exact percentage, because money invested in your 20s has the longest time to grow.

Is it too late to start contributing at 40 or 50?+

No. Starting later usually means contributing a higher percentage, often 15% to 20% or more. From age 50 you can also make catch-up contributions above the standard limit, which for 2027 is an extra $8,500 ($11,750 at ages 60 to 63).

How much does contributing to a 401(k) reduce my take-home pay?+

Less than you might think, if you use a Traditional 401(k). Because contributions are taken out before income tax, a $100 contribution might reduce your take-home pay by only about $78 if you're in the 22% federal tax bracket.
