Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate: Why Your Tax Bracket Isn't What You Think

Your marginal tax rate applies only to your last dollar of income; your effective rate is what you actually pay overall. Here's how both work, with 2026 brackets and real examples.

Your marginal tax rate is the rate on your last dollar of income — your “tax bracket.” Your effective tax rate is your total tax divided by your total income — what you actually pay on average. Because U.S. income tax is charged in layers, your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate. And moving into a higher bracket never makes all your income taxed at the higher rate.

“I turned down a raise because it would push me into a higher tax bracket” is a genuinely common misunderstanding — and one that can lead to real, unnecessary financial decisions based on a misconception about how income tax brackets actually work.

How tax brackets actually work

Under a progressive tax system, income is taxed in layers, or brackets, with each successive layer of income taxed at a higher rate — but only the income within that specific bracket is taxed at that bracket’s rate. Moving into a higher bracket doesn’t retroactively apply that higher rate to all of your income.

2026 federal tax brackets (single filers)

These apply to taxable income (income after deductions) for tax year 2026, which you file in 2027:

Rate Taxable income
10% $0 – $12,400
12% $12,401 – $50,400
22% $50,401 – $105,700
24% $105,701 – $201,775
32% $201,776 – $256,225
35% $256,226 – $640,600
37% Over $640,600

The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly. Thresholds are different for other filing statuses and are adjusted for inflation every year, so check IRS.gov for current figures.

A real example: $75,000 salary

Alex is single, earns $75,000, and takes the standard deduction.

Step 1: Find taxable income. $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900

Step 2: Tax each layer.

Layer Amount in layer Rate Tax
First $12,400 $12,400 10% $1,240
$12,401 – $50,400 $38,000 12% $4,560
$50,401 – $58,900 $8,500 22% $1,870
Total $58,900 $7,670
  • Marginal rate: 22%, because Alex’s last dollar falls in the 22% bracket.
  • Effective rate: $7,670 ÷ $75,000 ≈ 10.2% of total income.

Alex is “in the 22% bracket,” yet pays about 10% of their salary in federal income tax. Only $8,500 of their income is taxed at 22%.

Marginal tax rate

Your marginal tax rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income — 22% in Alex’s case. It’s the rate most commonly referenced when people talk about “my tax bracket,” and it’s the right number to use when estimating the tax on a change in income.

Effective tax rate

Your effective tax rate is your total tax divided by your total income — the real, blended average rate you actually paid. It’s meaningfully lower than the marginal rate, because most income is taxed at the lower brackets underneath. (Some people calculate it using taxable income instead of total income; either way it’s lower than the marginal rate.)

What happens when you get a raise

Say Alex gets a $5,000 raise to $80,000. That extra $5,000 still falls in the 22% bracket:

  • Extra federal income tax: $5,000 × 22% = $1,100
  • Extra Social Security and Medicare (FICA, 7.65%): about $383
  • Alex keeps about $3,517 of the raise (before any state tax)

Alex’s take-home pay goes up. Nothing about the first $75,000 is taxed differently. For a full breakdown of where each paycheck dollar goes, see understanding your paycheck.

Why this distinction matters

  • A raise never reduces your take-home pay because of brackets. Since only the additional income above your previous top bracket gets taxed at a higher rate, you always keep more from a raise than you did without it.
  • Your effective rate is usually the more meaningful number for understanding your overall tax burden.
  • Your marginal rate tells you what deductions are worth. A $1,000 Traditional 401(k) contribution saves Alex about $220 in federal tax, because it comes off the top 22% layer.
  • It helps with Roth vs. Traditional decisions. Comparing your marginal rate today with your expected rate in retirement is the core of the Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA decision.

The one real exception: benefit cliffs

Brackets themselves never make a raise cost you money. But some benefits and credits phase out or stop at certain income levels, such as health insurance premium subsidies, the Earned Income Tax Credit, or some public assistance programs. In those cases, a small raise can reduce benefits by more than the raise is worth. This is about losing benefits, not about tax brackets, and it affects a relatively small set of situations.

Where this gets more complicated

Real tax systems include additional factors this example doesn’t cover — different filing statuses, itemized deductions, tax credits, state and local income taxes, and different treatment for certain types of income, like qualified dividends and long-term capital gains, which have their own lower rates. The core principle (bracket layers apply only to the income within them) holds, but the exact numbers require checking current, official figures for your situation.

The bottom line

Income tax brackets apply in layers, not as a single flat rate on all your income once you cross a threshold. Your marginal rate (the rate on your top slice) is the one to use for decisions like raises and retirement contributions; your effective rate (your real average) shows your true overall burden. Understanding the difference clears up one of the most common and persistent misunderstandings about how taxes actually work.

Frequently asked questions/

What's the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?+

Marginal rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income (your top bracket). Effective rate is your total tax divided by your total income — the actual average rate you paid across all your income.

Will a raise push all my income into a higher tax bracket?+

No. Only the portion of income that falls within the higher bracket is taxed at that higher rate — the income below that threshold keeps being taxed at the lower rates that applied before.

Can a raise ever leave me with less take-home pay?+

Not because of tax brackets — only the additional income is taxed at the higher rate, so you always keep part of a raise. The rare exception is when extra income makes you lose an income-tested benefit or credit, such as certain health insurance subsidies or public benefits.

What are the 2026 federal tax brackets?+

For 2026 (taxes filed in 2027), the federal rates are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%. For single filers, the 12% bracket starts above $12,400 of taxable income, 22% above $50,400, 24% above $105,700, 32% above $201,775, 35% above $256,225, and 37% above $640,600.

Which tax rate should I use for financial decisions?+

Use your marginal rate to estimate the tax impact of a change, like a raise, a side job, or a Traditional 401(k) contribution. Use your effective rate to understand your overall tax burden.
