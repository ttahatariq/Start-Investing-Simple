The 12 Best Investing Books for Beginners (and Which to Read First)

The best investing books for beginners, from The Simple Path to Wealth to The Psychology of Money, with who each one is for and the order to read them.

The best investing books for beginners explain one big idea clearly: for most people, investing regularly in low-cost, diversified index funds and staying patient beats trying to pick winners. If you only read one, make it The Simple Path to Wealth by JL Collins or The Little Book of Common Sense Investing by John C. Bogle. Both are short, plain-spoken, and immediately useful.

Below are 12 books worth reading, grouped by what they’ll teach you, plus a suggested reading order. If you’d like a quick overview of your options before picking up a book, start with our guide to investments for beginners.

Start here: the “how to invest” books

1. The Simple Path to Wealth by JL Collins

Originally a series of letters to his daughter, this book lays out a complete plan in very simple terms: spend less than you earn, avoid debt, and invest the difference in a broad stock index fund. It also covers how to handle market crashes and how to think about retirement accounts.

Read it if: you want one book that tells you exactly what to do and why. Pairs with: our guides to index funds vs. individual stocks and understanding market volatility.

2. The Little Book of Common Sense Investing by John C. Bogle

Written by the founder of Vanguard and the creator of the first index fund for individual investors, this short book makes the case that costs matter enormously and that owning the whole market is the most reliable strategy for most people.

Read it if: you want to understand why low-cost index funds work. Pairs with: understanding expense ratios and what is an ETF?

3. I Will Teach You to Be Rich by Ramit Sethi

More hands-on than most investing books, this one walks you through setting up bank accounts, credit cards, retirement accounts, and automation step by step, then investing with low-cost funds. Its tone is casual and practical.

Read it if: you’re in your 20s or 30s and want a step-by-step setup guide. Pairs with: how to automate your savings and what is a brokerage account?

4. The Bogleheads’ Guide to Investing by Taylor Larimore, Mel Lindauer, and Michael LeBoeuf

Based on the philosophy of John Bogle and the online Bogleheads community, this is a practical manual covering asset allocation, retirement accounts, taxes, and keeping costs low.

Read it if: you want a reference book you’ll come back to. Pairs with: risk tolerance and asset allocation and how to choose your 401(k) investments.

5. If You Can by William J. Bernstein

A very short booklet originally written for young people starting their careers. It explains, in about an hour of reading, how to save, what to invest in, and what mistakes to avoid.

Read it if: you want the fastest possible starting point.

Then: the “how to think about money” books

6. The Psychology of Money by Morgan Housel

A collection of short chapters about how people actually behave with money: luck, risk, greed, patience, and what “enough” means. It contains very little technical investing advice, which is exactly why it’s so useful alongside a “how to invest” book.

Read it if: you want to understand why smart people make bad money decisions. Pairs with: lifestyle inflation and understanding market volatility.

7. Just Keep Buying by Nick Maggiulli

A data-driven look at common saving and investing questions, such as how much to save, whether to wait for a dip, and why consistency matters. Written by a data scientist, it backs up simple advice with numbers.

Read it if: you like seeing the evidence behind the advice. Pairs with: dollar-cost averaging vs. lump sum.

8. The Millionaire Next Door by Thomas J. Stanley and William D. Danko

Based on research into American millionaires, this classic shows that many wealthy people live below their means, drive ordinary cars, and built wealth slowly, not through flashy spending or high-risk bets.

Read it if: you need motivation that ordinary habits can build real wealth. Pairs with: needs vs. wants and what is net worth?

9. Your Money or Your Life by Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez

This book reframes money as “life energy” and asks whether your spending lines up with what you actually value. It’s less about investing and more about building the savings rate that makes investing possible.

Read it if: you want to rethink your relationship with spending. Pairs with: budgeting methods that actually work.

Later: the deeper classics

10. A Random Walk Down Wall Street by Burton G. Malkiel

First published in the 1970s and updated many times, this book explains how markets work, covers famous bubbles, and makes the evidence-based case that most investors are better off with index funds than with trying to beat the market.

Read it if: you’re curious about market history and the research behind index investing.

11. The Four Pillars of Investing by William J. Bernstein

Built around four ideas (investment theory, market history, investor psychology, and the investment industry), this book goes deeper into how stocks and bonds behave and how to build a portfolio. It’s more technical, but rewarding once you know the basics.

Read it if: you’ve read a beginner book or two and want real depth. Pairs with: stocks vs. bonds and international vs. domestic investing.

12. The Intelligent Investor by Benjamin Graham

Written by the man often called the father of value investing, and famously praised by Warren Buffett, this is the classic on investing with discipline instead of emotion. Look for the revised edition with commentary by Jason Zweig, which connects Graham’s ideas to modern markets.

Read it if: you want the foundational text, and you’re ready for a slower, denser read. Pairs with: growth vs. value investing.

A suggested reading order

If you’re starting from zero, this sequence builds understanding without overwhelming you:

  1. The Simple Path to Wealth or The Little Book of Common Sense Investing: learn the plan.
  2. The Psychology of Money: learn the mindset to stick with it.
  3. I Will Teach You to Be Rich: set up your accounts and automation.
  4. A Random Walk Down Wall Street: understand the evidence and history.
  5. The Intelligent Investor: go deep once the basics feel natural.

You don’t need to read all of them before you start investing. Reading one good book and opening a Roth IRA or raising your 401(k) contribution will do more for you than finishing a whole shelf.

How to get the most out of investing books

  • Borrow before you buy. Most of these are available at public libraries, often as free e-books and audiobooks through library apps.
  • Look for the newest edition. Several have been updated with current tax rules and market data.
  • Watch for outdated numbers. Contribution limits and tax rules change; for current figures, see our 2027 401(k) contribution limits.
  • Act on one idea. After each book, make one concrete change, like automating a monthly investment or lowering your fund fees.
  • Run the numbers. Our compound interest calculator shows what the books mean when they talk about “time in the market.”

Key takeaways

  • The best first investing books are The Simple Path to Wealth and The Little Book of Common Sense Investing.
  • Pair a “how to invest” book with a “how to think” book like The Psychology of Money.
  • Save dense classics like The Intelligent Investor for later.

Frequently asked questions/

What is the best investing book for beginners?+

For most beginners, The Simple Path to Wealth by JL Collins or The Little Book of Common Sense Investing by John C. Bogle is the best first book. Both explain low-cost index fund investing in plain language and are short enough to finish in a few evenings.

Should a beginner read The Intelligent Investor?+

Eventually, but probably not first. Benjamin Graham's The Intelligent Investor is a classic, but it's long and dense. Most beginners get more out of it after reading one or two simpler books on index investing and investor behavior.

Are investing books still worth reading in the age of YouTube and blogs?+

Yes. A good book gives you a complete, connected way of thinking about money that's hard to piece together from short videos and posts, and the best investing books have stayed relevant for decades.

What should I read after my first investing book?+

Pair a 'how to invest' book with a 'how to think' book. For example, follow The Simple Path to Wealth with The Psychology of Money, then move on to A Random Walk Down Wall Street or The Four Pillars of Investing for more depth.
