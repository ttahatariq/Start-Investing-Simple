Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA: Key Differences and Which to Choose

A Roth 401(k) has higher limits and no income cap; a Roth IRA has more investment choices and flexible withdrawals. Here's how they compare in 2027 and how to use both.

A Roth 401(k) and a Roth IRA work the same way on taxes: you contribute money you’ve already paid tax on, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. The differences are in everything else. A Roth 401(k) comes through your employer, has a much higher limit ($25,500 in 2027) and no income limits. A Roth IRA is an account you open yourself, has a lower limit ($7,500), and has income limits, but it gives you far more investment choices and more flexible access to your money.

You don’t have to choose one. Many people use both. Here’s how they compare and how to decide where your next dollar should go.

Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA: side-by-side

What they have in common

Both are “Roth” accounts, which means:

  • No tax deduction now. You contribute after-tax dollars.
  • Tax-free growth. Investment gains aren’t taxed while they stay in the account.
  • Tax-free qualified withdrawals. After age 59½, and once the account has met a five-year holding rule, everything comes out tax-free, including decades of growth.

That makes either one a strong choice if you expect to be in a similar or higher tax bracket in retirement, which is common for younger workers early in their careers. If you’re still deciding between Roth and pre-tax in the first place, start with our Traditional vs. Roth 401(k) and Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA guides.

The 5 key differences

1. Contribution limits

The Roth 401(k) wins on capacity. For 2027 you can contribute up to $25,500 to a Roth 401(k), more than three times the $7,500 Roth IRA limit. If you want to save a lot in a Roth account, the 401(k) is the only way to do it directly. Note that Roth and Traditional 401(k) contributions share the same $25,500 limit. Our 2027 401(k) contribution limits guide has the full breakdown, including catch-up amounts.

2. Income limits

This is the biggest practical difference. A Roth 401(k) has no income limit: anyone whose employer offers one can contribute, regardless of salary. A Roth IRA does: above a certain modified adjusted gross income, your allowed contribution shrinks and eventually drops to zero. The thresholds are adjusted every year, so check the current figures on IRS.gov.

For higher earners, the Roth 401(k) is often the simplest way to get Roth savings at all.

3. Employer match

Only the 401(k) can come with free money from your employer. Historically, matching contributions always went into a pre-tax (Traditional) account even if you contributed to the Roth side. Since the SECURE 2.0 Act, employers are allowed to offer Roth matching contributions, but many plans still deposit the match pre-tax. Either way, the match is the main reason to put money in your 401(k) first.

4. Investment choices

A Roth 401(k) limits you to the funds your employer picked, which might be excellent low-cost index funds or might be expensive actively managed funds. A Roth IRA, opened at a brokerage, lets you buy almost anything: index funds, ETFs, individual stocks, or bonds. If your 401(k) menu has high expense ratios, this flexibility is worth a lot over decades.

5. Access to your money

With a Roth IRA, you can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time, for any reason, with no tax or penalty, because you already paid tax on that money. That makes it a useful backstop, though it shouldn’t replace an emergency fund.

A Roth 401(k) is stricter. Most plans don’t allow withdrawals while you still work there, apart from loans or hardship withdrawals. Non-qualified withdrawals come out as a mix of contributions and earnings, so part of them can be taxed and penalized.

Required minimum distributions: no longer a difference

Roth 401(k)s used to require minimum withdrawals starting in your 70s, which was a big reason people rolled them into Roth IRAs. That changed in 2024: under SECURE 2.0, Roth 401(k)s no longer have required minimum distributions during the owner’s lifetime, the same as Roth IRAs.

The five-year rule (and a smart move)

Both accounts have a five-year rule: to withdraw earnings tax-free, the account must have been open for at least five years and you must be 59½ or older (or meet another exception).

The catch: the clocks are separate. If you roll a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA after leaving a job, the Roth IRA’s five-year clock is the one that applies. If you’ve never had a Roth IRA, that clock starts from scratch.

The smart move: open a Roth IRA and contribute even a small amount early in your career. That starts its five-year clock, so any future rollover is already “seasoned.”

Which should you choose?

For most people with a workplace plan, the answer is both, in this order:

  1. Roth 401(k) up to the full employer match. The match is an instant return you can’t get anywhere else.
  2. Roth IRA up to $7,500 (if your income allows). You get better investment choices and flexible access to contributions.
  3. Back to the Roth 401(k) with anything extra, up to $25,500.
  4. A regular brokerage account after that.

Lean more toward the Roth 401(k) if:

  • Your income is too high for a direct Roth IRA contribution
  • You want to save more than $7,500 a year in Roth accounts
  • Your plan offers low-cost index funds
  • You like the simplicity of automatic payroll contributions

Lean more toward the Roth IRA if:

  • Your 401(k) has limited or expensive fund options
  • You want access to your contributions in a pinch
  • You don’t have a workplace plan at all

Not sure how much to save overall? See how much you should contribute to your 401(k) for realistic benchmarks by age.

A quick example

Maya is 28, earns $70,000, and her employer matches 100% of contributions up to 4% of salary.

  • She puts 4% ($2,800) into her Roth 401(k) and gets a $2,800 match.
  • She contributes $7,500 to a Roth IRA and invests it in a total-market index fund.
  • That’s $10,300 of her own money in Roth accounts plus $2,800 from her employer, all growing toward tax-free retirement income.

If she gets a raise, the next dollars go back into the Roth 401(k). Our Roth vs. Traditional Calculator can show how the Roth choice plays out with your own tax rates.

Key takeaways

  • Same tax treatment: both use after-tax contributions with tax-free qualified withdrawals.
  • Roth 401(k): higher limit ($25,500), no income limit, possible employer match, limited fund menu.
  • Roth IRA: lower limit ($7,500), income limits, any investment you want, contributions withdrawable anytime.
  • Neither has RMDs for the original owner as of 2024.
  • Use both: capture the match in the 401(k), fund a Roth IRA, then go back to the 401(k).

Frequently asked questions/

What is the difference between a Roth 401(k) and a Roth IRA?+

Both are funded with after-tax money and allow tax-free qualified withdrawals in retirement. A Roth 401(k) is offered through your employer, has a much higher contribution limit ($25,500 in 2027) and no income limits. A Roth IRA is an account you open yourself, has a lower limit ($7,500 in 2027) and income limits, but offers far more investment choices and lets you withdraw your contributions at any time.

Can I have both a Roth 401(k) and a Roth IRA?+

Yes. The limits are separate, so in 2027 someone under 50 could contribute up to $25,500 to a Roth 401(k) and up to $7,500 to a Roth IRA, as long as their income allows a direct Roth IRA contribution.

Is a Roth 401(k) or Roth IRA better?+

Neither is better for everyone. A common approach is to contribute to your 401(k) up to the full employer match first, then fund a Roth IRA for its flexibility and investment choice, then return to the 401(k) if you can save more.

Do Roth 401(k)s have required minimum distributions?+

No, not anymore. Starting in 2024, the SECURE 2.0 Act eliminated required minimum distributions from Roth 401(k)s during the original owner's lifetime, matching the rule for Roth IRAs.

Can I roll a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA?+

Yes. When you leave your employer, you can generally roll a Roth 401(k) directly into a Roth IRA without paying taxes. Opening a Roth IRA early helps, because the Roth IRA's five-year clock is what applies after the rollover.
