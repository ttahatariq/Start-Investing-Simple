The True Cost of Minimum Payments on Credit Card Debt

See how paying only the minimum on credit card debt dramatically increases the total interest paid and how long it takes to become debt-free.

Credit card statements list a “minimum payment due” that’s often surprisingly small relative to the total balance. Paying only that minimum might feel manageable month to month, but it can quietly turn a moderate debt into a multi-year, high-cost burden.

How minimum payments are typically calculated

Credit card minimum payments are often calculated as a small percentage of your outstanding balance (commonly somewhere around 1-3%, though this varies by issuer) or a flat minimum dollar amount, whichever is greater. Critically, most of that payment goes toward covering the month’s accrued interest, with only a small remaining portion actually reducing your principal balance.

A simplified illustration

Suppose you have a $5,000 credit card balance at a 22% annual interest rate, and you only ever pay the minimum (assume a 2% minimum, gradually decreasing as the balance shrinks):

  • It could take well over a decade to pay off the balance in full.
  • The total interest paid over that time could end up exceeding the original balance itself — meaning you’d ultimately pay more in interest than the amount you originally borrowed.

The exact numbers depend on the specific interest rate and minimum payment formula, but the pattern holds broadly: minimum payments are structured in a way that can extend repayment for years and dramatically increase the total cost of the debt.

Why this happens

Credit card interest is typically compounded daily or monthly, meaning interest is calculated not just on your original purchases, but on previously accrued interest as well — the same compounding mechanic that makes investing so powerful, working in the opposite direction against you as a borrower. When your payment barely covers the interest charged, very little progress is made on the actual principal, and the cycle continues.

How paying more than the minimum changes the picture

Even modest increases above the minimum payment can dramatically shorten payoff time and reduce total interest paid, because more of each payment goes toward principal rather than interest. This is the same principle behind the extra-payment strategies covered in our debt payoff guide — additional payments beyond the minimum compound in your favor, accelerating payoff far more than the extra dollar amount alone might suggest.

Practical steps if you’re currently paying only minimums

  1. Calculate your actual payoff timeline using your card’s stated interest rate and current balance — many credit card statements are required to show an estimated payoff timeline at the minimum payment, which can be a sobering but clarifying number.
  2. Identify any amount you can add, even modestly, above the minimum — as shown above, even small increases can meaningfully shorten the payoff timeline.
  3. Consider whether a budgeting method could free up additional funds to direct toward debt payoff.
  4. Avoid adding new charges to the card while paying down the balance, since new purchases compound the problem rather than helping resolve it.
  5. Consider whether a balance transfer or debt consolidation option might reduce your interest rate — though these come with their own fees and terms worth carefully evaluating, ideally with attention to the underlying interest rate and any transfer fees involved.

Why minimum payments feel deceptively manageable

Minimum payments are, by design, calculated to be affordable in the short term — that’s part of why they can be dangerous over the long term. The immediate affordability can mask just how slowly they actually reduce the underlying balance, and how much the debt ultimately costs if minimums are paid indefinitely.

The bottom line

Paying only the minimum on credit card debt is one of the most expensive ways to manage a balance, often extending repayment for years and multiplying the total cost through compounding interest. Even a modest increase above the minimum payment — directed consistently — can meaningfully shorten that timeline and reduce the total interest paid.
